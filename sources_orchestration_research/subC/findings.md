### Toward Modular and Flexible Open RAN Implementations in 6G Networks: Traffic Steering Use Case and O-RAN xApps

- **Authors / Year / Venue / DOI / peer-reviewed or preprint**
  Marcin Dryjański (Rimedo Labs, Poznań, Poland), Łukasz Kułacz (Rimedo Labs; Institute of Radiocommunications, Poznan University of Technology), Adrian Kliks (Rimedo Labs; Institute of Radiocommunications, Poznan University of Technology — corresponding author, adrian.kliks@put.poznan.pl). **2021**. *Sensors* (MDPI), **vol. 21, issue 24, article 8173**, 10 pages. **DOI: 10.3390/s21248173**. PMID 34960268, PMCID PMC8705635. Received 20 Oct 2021, accepted 1 Dec 2021, published **7 December 2021**. **Peer-reviewed journal article** (Europe PMC `pubType: research-article, Journal Article`; MDPI `article-type="research-article"`), open access CC BY 4.0. **Not a preprint.** 24 references. Academic Editor: Omprakash Kaiwartya.

- **Type classification — pick EXACTLY ONE and justify in one sentence:**
  **(C) a controller.** The paper's primary original contribution is a set of three working xApps (Spectrum Management, Cell Assignment, Resource Allocation) that run in the O-RAN near-RT RIC and closed-loop control RAN radio-resource decisions, with a non-RT RIC ML component selecting which policy to apply — i.e. a RAN control application suite, not an orchestrator, not a generic optimization algorithm, and not a pure survey, even though roughly half the paper is an O-RAN overview/tutorial (Sections 1–3 + standardization activity summary) and the remaining half is original implementation plus simulation experiments.

- **Architecture**
  O-RAN hierarchical, three-control-loop architecture (the authors' own Figure 1/Figure 2):
  - **SMO / non-RT RIC** (>1 s loop): hosts rApps and the ML training/inference, holds the trained CCDF models, and issues policies to the near-RT RIC over **A1**. In this work the non-RT RIC additionally hosts the **logistic-regression policy selector**.
  - **near-RT RIC** (>10 ms, <1 s loop): hosts the xApps; provides an E2-node/cell/bearer/flow/UE configuration database, ML data-pipelining tools, messaging infrastructure between xApps and the RIC framework, logging/tracing/metrics toward SMO, xApp security functions, **xApp subscription management**, and a **conflict-mitigation function** to resolve overlapping xApp actions (the paper's example: MLB vs MRO causing a "ping-pong" effect).
  - **O-DU / E2 Nodes** (<10 ms RT loop): MAC scheduler is still real-time and only reachable by the near-RT RIC through general policies via **E2**.
  - BS decomposition into **O-CU / O-DU / O-RU** with **F1, E1, Open Fronthaul** interfaces (described, not implemented).
  - **Three xApps**, all mapped into the near-RT RIC (Figure 3): **xApp1 = Spectrum Management** (frequency-band selection, policies `CHEAP`, `PERFORMANCE`), **xApp2 = Cell Assignment** (UE→BS association, policies `DEFAULT`, `OFFLOADING`, `SEPARATING`), **xApp3 = Resource Allocation** (per-BS scheduling strategy, policies `EQUAL`, `PREFER_VOICE`, `PREFER_MBB`, `RESERVE`).
  - Policies are expressed as **declarative tuples stored in simple files such as JSON or YAML**, deliberately described as "creating an application programming interface (API)".

- **Control plane / management plane location**
  **O-RAN Alliance standard**, explicitly: the near-RT RIC sits in the RAN control plane and the **non-RT RIC sits inside the O-RAN Service Management and Orchestration (SMO)** framework. Specification references used by the paper: `O-RAN.WG3.RICARCH-v01.01` (Near-RT RIC Architecture), `O-RAN.WG2.AIML-v01.03` (AI/ML Workflow Description and Requirements), `O-RAN.WG2.Use-Case-Requirements-v02.01` (Non-RT RIC & A1 Interface: Use Cases and Requirements), `O-RAN.WG1.Slicing-Architecture-v05.00`.
  **No ETSI NFV MANO, no ETSI ZSM, no ONAP, no OSM, no 3GPP management-plane reference, and no Kubernetes control plane is used or implemented** (word-boundary counts in the full text for "ETSI", "NFV", "MANO", "ONAP", "OSM", "3GPP", "Kubernetes" are all 0).

- **Inputs** and **Outputs** (be concrete)
  **Inputs:** OSP/MNO-declared declarative policies (band-utilisation rules, macro-vs-small-cell preference, user-class priority) supplied as JSON/YAML tuples; the per-band MNO **cost `c`**; per-cell band capability; user class (voice vs MBB); strongest received power (for the `DEFAULT` baseline assignment rule); UE- and cell-specific metrics obtained from E2 nodes; non-RT measurements; previously trained CCDF performance models held in the non-RT RIC.
  **Outputs:** per-cell operating **frequency-band selection** (800 MHz vs 2 GHz); **UE-to-cell/BS assignment** decisions; per-user **bandwidth/resource allocation**; the resulting steering of user traffic across cells/bands; on the A1 path, the selected policy pushed from non-RT RIC to near-RT RIC; on the E2 path, control/policy messages to E2 nodes for resource allocation/prioritisation. Outputs are evaluated as mean bitrate per user class and outage probability.

- **Managed resources** (compute, network, radio, spectrum, VNFs, slices, pods...)
  **Radio resources and spectrum**: two carriers/bands (800 MHz with 5 MHz channel bandwidth; 2 GHz with 10 MHz bandwidth), cell selection/range-extension power offset, downlink resource blocks/bandwidth per user. **Cells and base stations**: 1 macro-BS + 4 small cells. **UEs/users** grouped into two service classes (voice, MBB). An abstract MNO **cost `c`** models energy/licence/load costs. **Not managed:** VNFs, network slices, pods, compute or transport resources (containerisation is discussed only as a software-delivery enabler).

- **Network domains**
  **RAN only — single domain.** No transport, core, cloud or multi-domain scope. Cloud/edge is invoked only rhetorically as the direction Open RAN software delivery is moving.

- **Interfaces/APIs**
  **A1** (non-RT RIC → near-RT RIC: policies, ML model management, feedback) — used conceptually and is central to the design. **E2** (near-RT RIC ↔ E2 Nodes: control/policy down, UE/RAN statistics up) — central to the design. **F1, E1, Open Fronthaul** (mentioned in the architecture overview of the disaggregated BS). **JSON/YAML policy files** exposed as a lightweight "API" for runtime policy modification. **NOT REPORTED / not used:** O1, O2, NETCONF/YANG, REST (a single passing "REST" mention only — no REST API is designed or implemented), TMF Open API, CAMARA, Kubernetes API.

- **Optimization engine** (exact method if reported)
  **No classical optimizer is reported** — no LP/MILP, no MDP/POMDP, no RL/DRL, no convex optimisation, no metaheuristic. The "optimisation" is **policy enumeration over a small discrete space** (xApp1: 2 policies × xApp2: 3 policies × xApp3: 4 policies, plus joint configurations such as xApp1+xApp2), with performance characterised by **complementary cumulative distribution functions (CCDF)** computed offline and then served to a **logistic-regression model that selects the policy which reduces outage in the system**. The paper states: "In our tests, we have applied a simple ML tool (sitting at the non-RT RIC)—logistic regression, which allows for the selection of the policy that reduces outage in the system."

- **ML/DRL/LLM/agent usage** (exact)
  **Logistic regression only**, located in the **non-RT RIC**, used as a policy selector minimising outage; its outputs ("trained models, or more precisely, achieved CCDFs") are "available in non-RT RIC and influence the policy choice by near-RT RIC". No DRL, no neural networks (DNN appears only in the background discussion of O-RAN AI/ML use cases: QoE prediction, cell load prediction, handover-anomaly detection, latency prediction), no LLM, no autonomous agent framework, no multi-agent system. There is **no online learning** in the evaluated system — the models are trained from prior CCDF experiments.

- **Centralized vs distributed; single vs multi-domain; closed-loop vs open-loop; static vs dynamic**
  **Hierarchical mix of centralised and distributed:** centralised non-RT/short-timescale policy authority in the SMO/non-RT RIC over a distributed set of near-RT xApps and per-node real-time schedulers. **Single-domain** (RAN, single operator, single HetNet deployment). **Closed-loop**, but a **slow, human/ML-mediated outer loop**: performance is measured → the operator or the logistic-regression selector picks a policy → the policy is applied → performance is re-measured; the inner RAN loops (near-RT via E2, RT MAC scheduling) are real-time. **Dynamic:** policies and even whole xApps are installed, changed, replaced and uninstalled at runtime — the experiments deliberately switch policies mid-scenario at the 50 s, 100 s and 150 s marks.

- **SLA awareness** (yes/no and how), **network slicing support**, **Kubernetes/cloud-native support**
  **SLA awareness: NO.** No SLA, SLO or SLI concept appears anywhere in the paper. Objectives are instead a three-way trade-off among mean bitrate for MBB users, mean bitrate for voice users, outage probability, and the abstract MNO cost `c`.
  **Network slicing: NOT IMPLEMENTED — discussed only as future work.** Slicing appears only in the Discussion, as a key prospective Open RAN application ("management of the network slices is one of the key applications of Open RAN, where dynamic setting up, modification, scaling and deleting of a network slice requires such an approach to virtualized and dynamically managed RAN"), supported by the O-RAN WG1 Slicing Architecture specification. No slice is created, managed or evaluated.
  **Kubernetes/cloud-native: DISCUSSED, NOT IMPLEMENTED.** Containerisation and CI/CD are presented as the key enabler of modular, continuously upgradable xApps, and the paper reasons about containerised, virtualised, "containerized" RAN software delivery — but the implementation itself is a plain custom simulator with no Kubernetes, no containers and no cloud-native runtime.

- **Evaluation environment** (simulator/emulator/testbed names; exact)
  **A self-written, generic, standalone simulation of a HetNet — no named simulator, emulator, testbed or open-source platform is used.** The paper is explicit: "our implementation is generic, i.e., we intentionally did not apply any of the existing platforms for RIC simulation. Our goal is to illustrate the benefits of xApp modularity supported by defined interfaces and the independence of the RIC platform."
  **Explicitly NOT REPORTED / not used:** Mininet, Containernet, Kubernetes, Open5GS, OSM, ONAP, OpenAirInterface, Colosseum, 5GinFIRE, ns-3, any O-RAN RIC platform (e.g. near-RT RIC open source), any hardware testbed, any over-the-air trial.
  Simulation configuration: HetNet with 1 high-power macro-BS + 4 small cells; two separated bands (800 MHz / 5 MHz channel bandwidth, and 2 GHz / 10 MHz bandwidth); **downlink only**; users uniformly distributed; 80 % voice users at ~250 kbps constant bit rate and the remaining 20 % MBB users at ~3 Mbps constant bit rate; a user is "in outage when its achieved bit rate is below the required one"; a default/reference setup of cheapest band + strongest-received-power assignment + equal resource allocation; results averaged over "numerous user location deployments" for the CCDFs, and time-series scenarios run over a 0–200 s window for a "random but fixed deployment of users".

- **Network scale** (number of nodes/slices/VNFs/domains if reported)
  **5 cells (1 macro-BS + 4 small cells)**, **2 frequency bands**, **2 user classes**, **3 xApps**, **3 experimentation scenarios**, **200 s per scenario**, policy changes at 50/100/150 s. **Number of simulated UEs: NOT REPORTED.** **Number of independent user-location deployments / Monte-Carlo iterations: NOT REPORTED** (described only as "numerous"). **1 domain.** Number of slices: **NOT REPORTED** (0 implemented). Number of VNFs: **NOT REPORTED** (none).

- **Baselines** and **Metrics** (exact)
  **Baseline:** the **default/reference setup** = the cheapest frequency band is selected + users assigned to the cell based on received signal power + radio resources allocated equally among users (i.e. the `CHEAP`/`DEFAULT`/`EQUAL` combination). Scenario 1 additionally starts with a "default, reference setup with no active xApp" before the first xApp is started at t = 50 s. No external or third-party baseline (no non-O-RAN baseline implementation, no alternative algorithm from the literature) is compared against.
  **Metrics (exact):** (i) **mean bitrate for MBB users** [Mbps]; (ii) **mean bitrate for Voice users** [Mbps]; (iii) **outage probability** (fraction of users whose achieved bit rate falls below their required bit rate), plotted on the right-hand axis of Figure 4. Also computed but shown as distributions rather than scalars: **CCDFs of observed rate and outage probability**, "averaged over numerous user location deployments".

- **Main quantitative results** (EXACT numbers, quote the paper)
  **No exact numeric performance results are reported anywhere in the paper.** All outcome statements are qualitative, and the numbers appear only as simulation input parameters or as axis ticks in Figure 4. The paper's own wording, quoted verbatim:
  - Scenario 1: "at the 50 s time stamp, xApp1 is started, with the default CHEAP policy (analogous to the default setup), and thus no changes are observed; at 100 s, the policy changes to PERFORMANCE, leading to **some mean bitrate increase** (for both MBB and Voice users) but at the expense of **some outage degradation**; at 150 s, the system returns to the prior setup."
  - Scenario 2: "at 50 s, xApp3 starts preferring MBB users over voice users, thus **the bitrate of MBB uses increases, and for Voice users, it decreases**, leading at the same time to an **improvement of outage probability**; at 100 s, xApp1 chooses PERFORMANCE, which in this network state … **causes performance degradation**; thus, the system selects again the CHEAP strategies at 150 s."
  - Scenario 3: "at 50 s, xApp starts preferring MBB users (**resulting in MBB bitrate increase at no other cost**), and at 100 s xApp2 selects the OFFLOADING policy (which is associated with **some additional cost for OSP, some deterioration of Voice bitrate but significant gain in outage probability**), whereas at 150 s, xApp1 returns to the CHEAP mode."
  - No percentage, no absolute Mbps value, no outage figure is quoted in the text for any scenario.
  **Numeric values that ARE in the paper (all inputs/axis ranges, not results):** 800 MHz @ 5 MHz bandwidth and 2 GHz @ 10 MHz bandwidth; 80 % voice / 20 % MBB; ~250 kbps and ~3 Mbps required rates; scenario timestamps 50/100/150 s over a 0–200 s x-axis; control-loop timescales RT <10 ms, near-RT >10 ms and <1 s, non-RT >1 s.
  **Read directly off Figure 4 (axis ranges only — NOT values stated in the paper's text, flagged as such):** left axis = "mean bitrate [Mbps]", right axis = "outage", x-axis = "time [s]" 0–200. Scenario 3's left axis is labelled 0.0–12.5 Mbps and its right axis 0.05–0.15; Scenarios 1 and 2 show a "mean bitrate [Mbps]" left axis and an outage right axis with ticks in the 0.10–0.20 range. The plotted traces show small step changes coinciding with the policy-switch markers A/B/C/D. **The paper itself derives no scalar from these curves.**

- **Main limitations** (as stated + methodological)
  **As stated by the authors:** (1) the study covers only **one use case** — "the authors provide a discussion on the case where all xApps operate for the purpose of a single use case", whereas real networks will have "multiple use cases to be served at the same time, e.g., interference management together with massive MIMO and QoE optimization with network slicing resource assurance to multiple slices, resulting in having multiple different xApps running in parallel"; (2) with many xApps controlling the same network part, "the role of the RIC itself becomes even more significant to avoid instability/oscillations in the network", and maintaining "conformance of installed applications" is an open challenge; (3) Open RAN "may be vulnerable to security threats, as the possibility of continuous and seamless updates of modules opens doors to various malicious activities"; (4) the RAN design process "has to be redefined in such a way that Open RAN-compliant software will achieve similar or better results than the existing solutions" — the paper does not demonstrate that; (5) xApp conflict mitigation (e.g. MLB vs MRO ping-pong) is acknowledged as necessary but is not implemented or evaluated here.
  **Methodological (this reviewer's assessment):** results are **purely qualitative** — no numeric results, no confidence intervals, no repetition count, no statistical significance, so the claimed benefits are not falsifiable from the paper; the **number of UEs and of Monte-Carlo deployments is not reported**, so the simulation cannot be reproduced; the authors deliberately bypass every existing O-RAN/RIC platform, so **no interoperability, E2/A1 protocol conformance, or real-time latency is demonstrated** — the "xApps" are logical roles in a bespoke simulator rather than deployable O-RAN xApps; the ML layer is a single logistic regression trained on the simulator's own CCDFs, with no online adaptation and no comparison against a learned baseline; the scope is single-domain RAN with no transport/core/slice/SLA dimension despite the 6G framing in the title; the "cost `c`" is abstract and never quantified, so the claimed OSP trade-offs are unquantified.

- **Research gap this suggests**
  There is no **quantitative, reproducible evaluation of multi-xApp conflict and arbitration on a standards-conformant O-RAN RIC** (real near-RT RIC + A1/E2), and no demonstrated mapping from RAN-level xApp control into an **end-to-end orchestrator** (SMO ↔ ETSI NFV MANO/ZSM ↔ slice manager). The "AI engine" is reduced to a single logistic-regression policy picker, so **learned, online, SLA- and slice-aware policy arbitration across many concurrently running xApps remains unaddressed**. The paper also shows a concrete gap between the Open RAN programmability narrative (CI/CD, containerised xApps, plug-and-play replacement) and the evaluated artefact (a bespoke simulator with no containers, no cloud-native runtime and no platform independence test) — i.e. **cloud-native xApp lifecycle orchestration for RAN is asserted but not built**.

- **Authors / Year / Venue / DOI / peer-reviewed or preprint**
  Rafael Vaño, Ignacio Lacalle (corresponding author, iglaub@upv.es), Carlos E. Palau — all Communications Department, Universitat Politècnica de València, 46022 Valencia, Spain; Piotr Sowiński — Systems Research Institute, Polish Academy of Sciences, ul. Newelska 6, 01-447 Warsaw, Poland, and Warsaw University of Technology, pl. Politechniki 1, 00-661 Warsaw, Poland; Raúl S-Julián — Communications Department, Universitat Politècnica de València. **2023**. *Sensors* (MDPI), **vol. 23, issue 4, article 2215**, 10 pages, 113 references. **DOI: 10.3390/s23042215**. PMID **36850813**, PMCID PMC9967903. Published **16 February 2023** (submitted 14 Jan 2023, revised 13 Feb 2023, accepted 14 Feb 2023). **Peer-reviewed review article** (Europe PMC `pubType: review-article, Review, Journal Article`; MDPI `article-type="review-article"`; article header literally reads "**Review**"), open access CC BY 4.0. **Not a preprint.** Academic Editor: Anfeng Liu. Funding: European Commission Horizon Europe project **aerOS**, grant 101069732; the authors also position the work within the **ASSIST-IoT** action.

- **Type classification — pick EXACTLY ONE and justify in one sentence:**
  **(G) a survey/tutorial (not a primary contribution).** The paper is explicitly a "deployment-first" review of container/edge workload orchestration tooling — it builds no orchestrator, runs no experiments and generates no data of its own (its own Data Availability Statement reads "No data were created or analyzed in this study"), its contribution being two comparative tables and a forward-looking assessment.

- **Architecture**
  The paper presents **no orchestration architecture of its own**; it contributes an **organisational taxonomy of the field** and reproduces the architectures of the systems it reviews (Figures 1–8). Its structure is:
  1. **Virtualisation layer** — VMs → serverless (OpenFaaS, Knative) → containers (Docker/Moby; OCI runtime-spec / image-spec / distribution-spec); abstracted as a two-tier **high-level runtime** (containerd, CRI-O) sitting above a **low-level runtime** (runC, crun, youki, runnc) (Figure 1).
  2. **Purpose-built edge operating systems** — EVE-OS (LF Edge; containerd + K8s on bare metal + VMs, managed by the Adam controller under a Zero-Trust model), BalenaOS (Yocto Linux + balenaEngine, up to 20 device types), Pantavisor (single-binary init system, micro-containers, ~350 KB footprint, 64 MB RAM minimum, with PantacorHub for management).
  3. **Commercial cloud-provider edge stacks** — AWS IoT Greengrass + AWS Snowball Edge, Azure IoT Edge + Azure Stack Edge (Pro / Pro R / Mini), Google Distributed Cloud.
  4. **Container orchestration for the edge, in two families:** (a) **lightweight K8s distributions used as edge clusters in a multi-cluster edge-to-cloud topology** (K3s, k3OS, MicroK8s, compared via Kubespray in [63]) — Figure 2a; (b) **frameworks that adapt the K8s architecture to the edge instead of shrinking it** (KubeEdge, OpenYurt, SuperEdge, Open Horizon, Baetyl, Project Flotta, Eclipse ioFog) — Figure 2b. **KubeEdge** is presented as a **three-layer architecture: Cloud (K8s + CloudCore controllers) / Edge (EdgeCore, no K8s API or control plane, MQTT broker, device mappers; EdgeMesh as a separate service-mesh component) / Devices (leaf devices via mappers)** — Figures 3 and 4. **Open Horizon** = Management Hub (cloud, requires K8s) + Edge Agent (Edge Device Agent or Edge Cluster Agent). **Eclipse ioFog** = cloud controller + edge agents ("Edge Compute Network"). **Akri** is covered separately as a K8s Resource Interface exposing leaf devices (ONVIF, udev, OPC-UA handlers) and is deliberately excluded from the comparison table.
  5. **Beyond containers** — microVMs/lightweight VMs (Kata Containers, RunD), Unikernels (MirageOS, Nabla), and **WebAssembly + WASI** with a server-side execution architecture (Figure 6) and Wasm runtimes (Table 2); **joint orchestration of heterogeneous workloads inside Kubernetes** via the **Runtime Class** mechanism, `containerd-wasm-shim`, and Krustlet (Figure 7, Figure 8).
  Control-loop description: **NOT REPORTED** — the review describes no control loop of its own.

- **Control plane / management plane location**
  **Kubernetes control plane** throughout, in the various placements the reviewed systems choose: **KubeEdge keeps the entire K8s control plane in the cloud and leaves only the application plane (workloads) at the edge**, with the edge node not being a K8s node (no K8s API, no control plane) and with control-plane information cached at the edge/intermediate nodes for disconnected operation; **Open Horizon** keeps workload and device management in a centralised cloud **Management Hub** with an **Edge Agent** at the edge; **Eclipse ioFog** uses a cloud controller + edge agents; **K3s/MicroK8s** create a **multi-cluster** topology where a full K8s deployment in the cloud coexists with lightweight distributions at the edge.
  **Standards explicitly NOT referenced anywhere in the paper (word-boundary counts = 0): ETSI NFV MANO, ETSI ZSM, ONAP, OSM, O-RAN SMO, 3GPP, network slicing, SLA.** MEC is mentioned exactly once, in passing, as an adjacent literature area ("mobile edge computing (MEC) … mainly addressed by telecom operators more concerned to 5G innovations"). Telco/5G appears only inside the Google Distributed Cloud description (virtualising telcos' 5G network elements) and in the KubeEdge bridge use case (a "reliable 5G connection").

- **Inputs** and **Outputs** (be concrete)
  **Inputs (of the review process):** the authors' own scoping of the deployment problem; searches in "usual article databases considering the target knowledge domain (such as IEEE, Elsevier, Springer, Google Scholar)" filtered to reviews/analyses; plus, in parallel, **non-scientific sources** — open repositories (e.g. GitHub tools and reference collections), research-project deliverables and whitepapers, and material from CNCF and the Alliance for Internet of Things Innovation (AIOTI). Items were categorised by type of virtualisation, commercial vs open, orchestration focus, workload-deployment focus, and current-vs-future orientation. **No search strings, database dates, hit counts, or inclusion/exclusion criteria are reported.**
  **Outputs:** a narrative deployment review (Sections 3–7); **Table 1** comparing 7 container-orchestration technologies for the edge; **Table 2** comparing 8 WebAssembly runtimes; 8 architecture figures; and a conclusion identifying **WebAssembly + joint heterogeneous-workload orchestration as "the topics to pay attention to in the short to medium term"** and KubeEdge as the most promising edge K8s adaptation. **No runtime control output of any kind.**

- **Managed resources** (compute, network, radio, spectrum, VNFs, slices, pods...)
  **Compute workloads and their packaging**: containers/pods, microVMs (Kata Containers), Unikernels, WebAssembly modules; container/high-level/low-level runtimes; OCI images and registries. **Edge and cloud nodes and clusters**: K8s clusters, server/agent nodes, multi-cluster federations; CPU-architecture support is a comparison criterion (x86_64, ARMv8/ARMv7, RISC-V, MIPS, XTENSA, ppc64le, i386). **Edge devices and fleets**: managed device fleets (Balena Cloud, PantacorHub, Adam controller, Open Horizon Management Hub), IoT leaf devices exposed via device mappers/Discovery Handlers (MQTT, Modbus, OPC-UA, ONVIF, udev), and edge **OS images/firmware** (EVE-OS, BalenaOS, Pantavisor, k3OS). **Network**: CNI, service mesh (EdgeMesh, Raven, ServiceGroups) and secure tunnels for cloud↔edge connectivity, Cilium multi-cluster networking. **NOT managed / absent: radio, spectrum, RAN functions, VNFs, network slices.** Pods are the central managed resource.

- **Network domains**

- **Main limitations** (as stated + methodological)
  **As stated by the authors:** (1) the field lacks grounding — during their EC projects "there has not been enough grounding or proof of success to consolidate a single reference yet", and "there is not yet a de facto standard equivalent for K8s on the edge"; (2) containers have real weaknesses that worsen at the edge — "large image size, non-robust security, and low isolation in the case of multi-tenant applications"; (3) microVMs remain unproven — "no clear results to date would demonstrate this reliably"; (4) Unikernels carry "many disadvantages, the most prominent being the lack of standardization … in addition to the need of a complete library operating system rebuild for every new application with any minimal changes, followed by the limitation of debugging and monitoring capabilities"; (5) multi-cluster tooling is not edge-ready — "the inner mechanisms of multi-cluster tools (e.g., communication, networking, high-availability, services discovery…) are not currently adapted to the particularities of edge computing"; (6) WASI is immature — "the most mature proposals are in phase 2"; (7) commercial cloud-vendor edge stacks create vendor lock-in and "most of these frameworks are not actually edge-native solutions"; (8) Baetyl "is in a preliminary stage with a clear lack of documentation"; (9) they acknowledge that the field's advances often bypass formal publication, which forces a grey-literature-dependent method.
  **Methodological (this reviewer's assessment):** (a) **no systematic review protocol** — no PRISMA, no search strings, no database/date coverage, no hit counts, no inclusion/exclusion criteria, no screening or quality appraisal, so the selection of the 7 + 8 compared technologies is not reproducible or demonstrably exhaustive; (b) **no benchmarking of its own**, despite being a "deployment-first" review whose central claim is about deployability — the performance numbers are inherited from heterogeneous third-party tests with different hardware and workloads, so they are not comparable across rows; (c) **Table 1/Table 2 cells are undated and volatile** — GitHub star/fork counts and "Production-Stage Deployments" status are snapshots with no as-of date, and "Popularity" is a weak proxy for orchestration suitability; (d) **telecom orchestration standards are entirely absent** (0 mentions of ETSI NFV MANO/ZSM, ONAP, OSM, 3GPP, O-RAN), so the review cannot speak to the operator/5G domain that a network-orchestration literature review most needs; (e) **no SLA, QoS, latency or slicing dimension** at all, leaving the "orchestration" framing purely a placement/packaging question; (f) the conclusion that Wasm is "the frontrunner for the next generation" rests on cited benchmarks and a Hykes quote rather than on the authors' own measurement.

- **Research gap this suggests**
  The review's own findings imply a concrete gap set: there is **no single consolidated open reference stack for orchestrating heterogeneous workloads (container + microVM + Unikernel + Wasm) across the edge-to-cloud continuum**, and no agreement on whether the K8s control plane should stay centralised in the cloud (KubeEdge/Open Horizon/ioFog model) or be federated as multi-cluster (K3s/MicroK8s model) — with **federated management domains and unified orchestration for heterogeneous systems named as open issues**. Because the paper never touches ETSI/3GPP/O-RAN orchestration standards, it also exposes a **gap in the literature itself**: cloud-native edge orchestration and standards-based network/NFV/SMO orchestration are reviewed in disjoint bodies of work, so an **SLA-, QoS- and slice-aware, AI-driven placement layer spanning edge/cloud *and* the network domains (RAN/transport/core) remains unaddressed**, as does **rigorous, reproducible benchmarking of edge orchestrators under a common methodology** (the review can only cite incomparable third-party numbers).
