## Paper 1

### Network Service Orchestration: A survey
- **Authors / Year / Venue / DOI / peer-reviewed or preprint:** Nathan F. Saraiva de Sousa, Danny A. Lachos Perez, Raphael V. Rosa, Mateus A. S. Santos, Christian Esteve Rothenberg. **Computer Communications (Elsevier), vol. 142-143, pp. 69-94, June 2019.** DOI **10.1016/j.comcom.2019.04.008**. Peer-reviewed journal article. The full text read is the author preprint arXiv:1803.06596v4 (17 May 2019), whose header states "Preprint submitted to Computer Communications".
- **Type classification:** **(G) survey/tutorial (not a primary contribution).** It is explicitly a survey that reviews background, standardization, research projects, open-source solutions and proposes a taxonomy, with no new system built or evaluated by the authors.
- **Architecture:** The survey does not propose a system architecture; it organizes the field. It defines three primary orchestrator functional scopes forming a layered view: **Service Orchestration (SO)** — service composition/decomposition, the upper layer interacting with Marketplace and OSS/BSS; **Lifecycle Orchestration (LO)** — management of workflows, processes and dependencies across service components, maintaining services per the contracted SLA; and **Resource Orchestration (RO)** — mapping service requests onto virtual and/or physical resources, across elements such as NFVO, EMS and SDN controllers. It states an orchestrator "may be inserted in each layer of telecommunication network stack, from the application layer down to the data plane" and that "different orchestrators can exist in each plane, not being limited to a single orchestrator." It distinguishes *Lifecycle* (sequence of states) from *Workflow* (activities in correct order).
- **Control plane / management plane location:** ETSI NFV MANO is the central reference. The survey notes ETSI ISG NFV defines the MANO architectural framework and that "NFVO takes a fundamental role in NFV-MANO functional components… realizing: (i) the orchestration of infrastructure resources (including multiple VIMs), fulfilling the Resource Orchestration functions, (ii) and the management of Network Services." NSO is placed "at a higher level in the control and management stack with interfaces to the OSS/BSS." It also covers MEF LSO, TM Forum, IETF, OASIS (TOSCA), ONF, ITU, NGMN.
- **Inputs and Outputs:** INPUTS: service requests / network service descriptors, customer and vertical demands, resource availability in virtual and/or physical infrastructure, SLA contracts, higher-level objectives from OSS/BSS. OUTPUTS: composed/decomposed network services, resource mappings, lifecycle workflows (e.g., scaling, topology/performance management, automation) propagated to MANO elements, service state toward OSS/BSS.
- **Managed resources:** Compute, storage and network resources across heterogeneous infrastructures; VNFs; virtual and physical network functions; multi-VIM infrastructure; slices (as "Slice as a Service", SlaaS, is discussed as a service model); transport/optical and data-centre resources.
- **Network domains:** Explicitly multi-domain. Application scenarios covered: Next-Generation Mobile Telecommunication Networks (5G), Transport Networks, Cloud Data Centers, Internet of Things. It distinguishes single-domain from multi-domain (both multi-technology and multiple administrative domains) orchestration.
- **Interfaces/APIs:** REST-based interfaces are identified as dominant ("The majority of solutions use REST-based …"); CLI, API and GUI are enumerated as management interface types; TOSCA (Simple Profile in YAML v1.2; TOSCA for NFV v1.0) for service modelling; NETCONF and YANG (the UNIFY project's YANG model named "Virtualizer" with configurations "following the NETCONF protocol"); OpenFlow extensions for SDN/optical. ETSI multi-administrative-domain reference points: **Umbrella NFVO** and **Or-Or**.
- **Optimization engine:** NOT REPORTED as a specific method — the survey is taxonomy-level and does not define or evaluate an optimizer.
- **ML/DRL/LLM/agent usage:** NOT REPORTED as a primary contribution (the survey does not itself apply ML; it is written before the DRL wave and covers orchestration platforms/standards).
- **Centralized vs distributed; single vs multi-domain; closed-loop vs open-loop; static vs dynamic:** The survey observes that "Some of the existing orchestration solutions use an orchestrator logically centralized and consider only 'softwarized' networks." It covers both single-domain (domain orchestrator with total control inside administrative boundaries, working "as described by ETSI") and multi-domain (local orchestrators do not know other providers' resources/topologies; requires cross-domain information exchange, for which **"there is not a standard"**). Dynamic/slice lifecycle orientation; loop semantics not formalized.
- **SLA awareness:** Yes, conceptually — Lifecycle Orchestration "maintains the services running according to the contracted Service Level Agreement"; SLA is also discussed in the Cloud Data Center scenario for guarantees across distinct service classes. How it is enforced technically: NOT REPORTED.
- **Network slicing support:** Yes — SlaaS is listed as a service model where "the slices are traded and used to build infrastructure services"; network slicing is referenced in the 5G scenario and via the referenced survey [21].
- **Kubernetes/cloud-native support:** Partially — Kubernetes is mentioned as a computer-virtualization platform providing networking extensions/plugins for dynamic networking between servers, and cloud-native/open-source orchestration projects (Cloudify, ONAP, CloudStack, OpenStack, Docker/container ecosystems) are surveyed. Not a cloud-native orchestrator design.
- **Evaluation environment:** NOT REPORTED (no simulator/emulator/testbed was run by the authors; surveys of projects and platforms are qualitative).
- **Network scale:** NOT REPORTED.
- **Baselines and Metrics:** NOT REPORTED.
- **Main quantitative results:** NOT REPORTED (no original measurements). Note: the survey does report others' standardization outcomes in Table 2 (ETSI NFV ISG Releases, MEF LSO, TM Forum, IETF) but no numbers of its own.
- **Main limitations:** As stated: the authors note the field moves fast and describe the work as a "living document" inviting community feedback via GitHub; they observe that open-source orchestration platforms "require to evolve until become suitable for production." Methodological (our assessment): the taxonomy is qualitative and the platform comparison is a snapshot that is now dated (pre-2020); no quantitative benchmarking of the surveyed orchestrators; ML/AI-driven orchestration is essentially absent from the coverage.
- **Research gap this suggests:** The survey establishes that multi-domain information exchange had no standard and that orchestrators are largely logically centralized over "softwarized" infrastructure — leaving open (a) standardized cross-domain intent/policy exchange, (b) closed-loop SLA enforcement with measurable guarantees, and (c) an AI/ML control layer above MANO, which is precisely the gap the later AI-native literature fills.
- **Best URL actually retrieved, and whether full text or abstract:** https://arxiv.org/pdf/1803.06596v4 — **FULL TEXT** (33-page author preprint). Published version: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.comcom.2019.04.008

## Paper 2

### 5G Network Slicing Using SDN and NFV: A Survey of Taxonomy, Architectures and Future Challenges
- **Authors / Year / Venue / DOI / peer-reviewed or preprint:** Alcardo Alex Barakabitze, Arslan Ahmad, Rashid Mijumbi, Andrew Hines. **Computer Networks (Elsevier), vol. 167, art. 106984, 2020** (received 21 Apr 2019; accepted 6 Nov 2019; available online 17 Nov 2019). DOI **10.1016/j.comnet.2019.106984**. Peer-reviewed journal article, **open access under CC BY-NC-ND**. The full text read is the arXiv preprint 1912.02802v1, which is the accepted-manuscript layout of the published article.
- **Type classification:** **(G) survey/tutorial (not a primary contribution).** It is a review/tutorial ("we provide a comprehensive review and updated solutions… a tutorial of 5G network slicing technology enablers… we comprehensively survey different industrial initiatives and projects"), with no new system implemented or measured by the authors.
- **Architecture:** Review-level. It presents the **ETSI NFV MANO reference architecture** as functional blocks grouped into NFV Infrastructure (NFVI), NFV Management and Orchestration, Network Management System, and VNFs and Services, connected by reference points; ETSI additionally includes **two SDN controllers** — the **Infrastructure SDN Controller (ISDNC)**, which provides connectivity for VNFs and is managed by the VIM, and the **Tenant SDN Controller (TSDNC)**, instantiated in the tenant domain as a VNF or part of the NMS. "Each controller provides a different level of abstraction. While the TSDNC provides an overlay comprising tenant VNFs… the ISDNC provides an underlay to support the deployment and connectivity of VNFs." The survey adds a domain-based decomposition: **single-domain management and orchestration**, **multi-domain orchestration and management**, **network slicing management in edge/cloud and fog computing**, and **RAN slicing for multi-service 5G networks**.
- **Control plane / management plane location:** ETSI NFV MANO is the anchor (NFVO/VNFM/VIM); two-controller SDN split (ISDNC/TSDNC); standardization efforts surveyed include ETSI, ITU-T, ONF, BBF and IETF; 3GPP architecture referenced for slice management. Open-source orchestrators surveyed: OSM, OpenMANO, OpenNFV, CloudNFV, OpenBaton, Cloudify, T-NOVA, OPNFV, ExperiaSphere, M-CORD, ZOOM, NGSON, ONAP.
- **Inputs and Outputs:** INPUTS: vertical/service requirements (data rate, ultra-low latency, availability, security, mobility, QoE, battery life), tenant slice requests, infrastructure resource state, ELA/SLA requirements, slice templates. OUTPUTS: instantiated isolated logical networks (slices) with dedicated resources; VNF placement and chaining decisions; end-to-end slice lifecycle operations across domains; QoE-based service billing/pricing signals.
- **Managed resources:** Physical and virtual network resources; radio/RAN resources (RAN slicing, virtual baseband units); compute and storage (VMs and containers); spectrum (two-level spectrum allocation described from the literature); VNFs; network slices; MEC/cloud/fog resources; transport resources.
- **Network domains:** RAN, transport, core, edge/cloud/fog, and multi-domain (multi-operator/multi-technology). Section 7.2 states multi-domain orchestrators "handle the life-cycle management of E2E slices across multiple administrative domains while domain-specific orchestrators build slices of the network, compute, and storage resources."
- **Interfaces/APIs:** Southbound programmable interfaces implementing **OpenFlow, NETCONF and I2RS** (explicitly named for the ISDNC/TSDNC); ETSI MANO reference points; NFVO/VNFM/VIM interfaces. TMF/CAMARA/A1/E2/O1/O2: NOT REPORTED (the paper predates the O-RAN interface focus).
- **Optimization engine:** NOT REPORTED as an original contribution. The survey mentions, from the literature, a Hap-SliceR radio-resource-slicing framework "based on the reinforcement learning approach that considers slice utility requirements" (attributed to Aijaz [306]) — reported as related work, not produced by the authors.
- **ML/DRL/LLM/agent usage:** Essentially absent as a first-class theme. Only passing literature references (e.g., the RL-based Hap-SliceR framework) and author-biography mentions of machine learning. No LLM/agent content (paper predates LLMs).
- **Centralized vs distributed; single vs multi-domain; closed-loop vs open-loop; static vs dynamic:** Covers both single-domain (domain orchestrator with full control inside its administrative boundary) and multi-domain (requires cross-domain information exchange; explicitly stated that no standard exists for that exchange). Dynamic on-demand slice creation ("slices of virtual mobile networks are created on-demand and customized according to the changing needs of mobile services"). Loop semantics not formalized by the authors.
- **SLA awareness:** Yes — SLA/ELA compliance is a recurring theme: multi-domain work is described as monitoring "service for ELA/SLA compliance over 5G networks"; multi-domain requirement is to map service requests "while matching each service ELA requirements"; the survey notes slice resource amounts derive from SLAs. Enforcement mechanism: NOT REPORTED.
- **Network slicing support:** Yes — this is the entire subject of the paper (slice definitions/history, taxonomy, single/multi-domain slice management, RAN slicing, edge/cloud/fog slice management).
- **Kubernetes/cloud-native support:** Partially. Containers and container-based virtualization are surveyed (Docker, Linux-VServer, OpenVZ, Oracle Solaris Containers) as lightweight alternatives to hypervisor VMs capable of running VNFs chained together. Kubernetes is mentioned specifically in the ONAP description: ONAP "leverages cloud-native technologies including Kubernetes to manage and rapidly…". No cloud-native orchestrator designed by the authors.
- **Evaluation environment:** NOT REPORTED (no simulator/emulator/testbed run by the authors; it reviews others' PoCs and projects: T-NOVA, MATILDA, SliceNet, SONATA, NECOS, 5G!PAGODA, 5GEx, and PoCs).
- **Network scale:** NOT REPORTED.
- **Baselines and Metrics:** NOT REPORTED.
- **Main quantitative results:** NOT REPORTED. (The only numeric statement encountered is a cited industry forecast that "Mobile video traffic alone will account for 78% of the global" traffic — attributed to an external source, not a result of this paper.)
- **Main limitations:** As stated: the survey lists open challenges — network sharing and slicing in 5G, end-to-end slice orchestration and management, security and privacy, RAN virtualization, business-model/economic challenges, mobility management, and OTT-ISP collaboration for QoE-based service management — i.e., the authors acknowledge these are unsolved. Methodological: the work is a 2019-vintage qualitative review; its orchestrator comparison (OSM, ONAP, OpenBaton, etc.) is descriptive and not benchmarked; the AI/ML control layer is not treated systematically; O-RAN and cloud-native CNF orchestration are out of scope.
- **Research gap this suggests:** The survey documents that E2E slice orchestration across administrative domains, RAN virtualization, and mobility management remain open, and that automation is largely manual/platform-specific — a clear gap for AI-native, cross-domain, closed-loop slice management with standardized intent interfaces.
- **Best URL actually retrieved, and whether full text or abstract:** https://arxiv.org/pdf/1912.02802v1 — **FULL TEXT** (40 pages). Published version: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.comnet.2019.106984 (CC BY-NC-ND).

## Paper 5

### Knowledge-Defined Networking
- **Authors / Year / Venue / DOI / peer-reviewed or preprint:** Albert Mestres, Alberto Rodriguez-Natal, Josep Carner, Pere Barlet-Bas, Eduard Alarcón, Marc Solé, Victor Muntés-Mulero, David Meyer, Sharon Barkai, Mike J. Hibbett, Giovani Estrada, Khaldun Ma'ruf, Florin Coras, Vina Ermagan, Hugo Latapie, Chris Cassar, John Evans, Fabio Maino, Jean Walrand, Albert Cabellos. **ACM SIGCOMM Computer Communication Review (CCR), vol. 47, no. 3, pp. 2-10, July/September 2017** (published 6 Sep 2017). DOI **10.1145/3138808.3138810**. Peer-reviewed journal article (ACM CCR). Full text read: arXiv:1606.06222v2 (a preprint of the same paper; the CCR version is the version of record).
- **Type classification:** **(D) an orchestration architecture/framework design.** It proposes the KDN paradigm — an architectural blueprint stitching SDN + Network Analytics + ML into a control loop — and validates it with two proof-of-concept use cases rather than shipping a complete production orchestrator.
- **Architecture:** A three-stage closed control loop (Fig. 2) layered above SDN:
 1. **Forwarding Elements & SDN Controller → Analytics Platform.** The Analytics Platform monitors data-plane elements in real time and queries the SDN controller for control/management state, using NETCONF (RFC 6241), NetFlow (RFC 3954) and IPFIX (RFC 7011). Data categories collected: packet-level/flow-level data (DPI, flow granularity, traffic features); **network state** (physical, topological, logical configuration); **control & management state** (policy, virtual topologies, application info); **service-level telemetry** (service load, QoE); **external information** (social networks, weather forecasts). A **historical record** of collected data is retained.
 2. **Analytics Platform → Machine Learning.** "The heart of the KP are the ML and Deep Learning algorithms." Three approaches are enumerated: **supervised learning** (learn a model relating network variables to network operation — e.g., performance as a function of traffic load and configuration), **unsupervised learning** (structure discovery, e.g., how local weather affects link utilization), and **reinforcement learning** (an agent acts on the SDN controller, receiving rewards as the in-place policy approaches a target policy, e.g. a delay target for a set of flows). Learning may occur offline and be applied online.
 3. **Machine Learning → Intent Language.** The KP expresses control decisions via an **Intent-driven language**, "which eases the transition between the high-level decisions made by the KP and the imperative language used by data plane elements."
 Applications are tabulated by learning type against **Closed Loop / Open Loop**: supervised → Automation, Optimization and (open loop) What-if analysis; unsupervised → Improvement (closed) and Recommendation (open); reinforcement → Automation and Optimization (closed), N/A for open loop.
- **Control plane / management plane location:** The Knowledge Plane is layered on top of **SDN** (logically centralized SDN controller programming the data plane) and the existing **management plane**; the KP "processes the network analytics collected by the management plane, transforms them into knowledge via ML." It revives and operationalizes D. Clark's "Knowledge Plane for the Internet." No ETSI NFV MANO / ZSM / ONAP / O-RAN binding is specified — the framework is technology-agnostic SDN+analytics.
- **Inputs and Outputs:** INPUTS: real-time packet/flow telemetry and DPI from forwarding elements; SDN controller control and management state (policy, virtual topologies, application info); network physical/topological/logical configuration; service-level telemetry (service load, QoE); external data (social networks, weather forecasts); operator intent/target policies (e.g., a delay target for a set of flows). OUTPUTS: a learned network model ("knowledge"); recommendations; control decisions expressed in an Intent-driven language, pushed to the SDN controller as configuration updates.
- **Managed resources:** Network forwarding resources and flows; overlay/underlay topology and routing configuration; VNF/VM placement and CPU/memory consumption in NFV scenarios; energy consumption; generally "compute, storage, and network" via SDN/NFV programmability.
- **Network domains:** Primarily a single network domain (an overlay network over an underlay; an NFV deployment in a data-centre/ISP setting). Multi-domain orchestration is NOT the subject. Domains touched: transport/overlay network and cloud/data-centre (NFV).
- **Interfaces/APIs:** **NETCONF (RFC 6241)**, **NetFlow (RFC 3954)**, **IPFIX (RFC 7011)** for telemetry/config collection; **SDN controller northbound Intent interface** for closed-loop actuation; Open vSwitch and Snort as instrumented elements in the experiments. A1/E2/O1/O2, TMF Open API, CAMARA, Kubernetes API: NOT REPORTED.
- **Optimization engine:** NOT REPORTED as a named solver. The paper states the learned model "can be explored through common optimization techniques to find (quasi)optimal configurations" (closed-loop optimization) without specifying an algorithm. Experiments use: an **Artificial Neural Network (ANN)** with one hidden layer (sigmoid activation) trained via **Pylearn2–Theano 0.7** for the overlay-delay model; and the **Matlab ANN toolbox** with one hidden layer for the VNF CPU-consumption model.
- **ML/DRL/LLM/agent usage:** **ML is the core contribution.** Explicitly: supervised, unsupervised and reinforcement learning as the knowledge-generation mechanism; **deep reinforcement learning** cited as a recent breakthrough enabler ("Recently, deep reinforcement learning techniques have provided important breakthroughs in the AI field, notable examples are [21], [22]"). No LLM usage (2017 paper) and no multi-agent design. RL is described at the paradigm level (agent acts on the SDN controller, receives reward as policy approaches target) rather than implemented.
- **Centralized vs distributed; single vs multi-domain; closed-loop vs open-loop; static vs dynamic:** Conceptually **centralized** Knowledge Plane (like the logically centralized SDN controller), operating a **closed loop** for automation/optimization and an **open loop** for recommendation/what-if analysis. **Single-domain.** **Dynamic** by construction (continuous telemetry, continual model refinement). Learning can be offline (trained on large datasets of behaviour) and applied online.
- **SLA awareness:** No formal SLA object. The closest constructs are a **target policy** set by the network administrator (the paper's example is "the delay of a set of flows") used as the RL reward target, and service-level telemetry (QoE). Explicit SLA contracts: NOT REPORTED.
- **Network slicing support:** NOT REPORTED (the term network slicing is not a design element; the NFV use case concerns VNF placement for resource management).
- **Kubernetes/cloud-native support:** NOT REPORTED.
- **Evaluation environment:** Two proof-of-concept experiments, both *partial* (they validate the "learn a model" step, not the full closed loop):
 1. **Overlay routing:** a simulated network with **12 overlay nodes, 19 underlay elements and 72 links**, built in **Omnet++**; overlay nodes split traffic randomly; the underlay uses shortest-path routing with constant link capacity, constant propagation delay and Poisson traffic. **Pylearn2–Theano 0.7** ANN (one hidden layer, sigmoid). Training with **9,600 training samples** and **300 separate validation samples**. The ANN has no access to underlay information.
 2. **NFV resource management:** **Open vSwitch (OVS) v2.0.2** and **Snort v2.9.6.0** deployed in VMs (**Ubuntu 14.04.1 on VMware ESXi v5.5**), connected by gigabit links to two traffic generator/receiver VMs. Traffic replayed with **tcpreplay v3.4.4** from an on-campus DPI infrastructure serving ~**30k users**. **86 traffic features** extracted offline in **20-second batches**. **Matlab ANN toolbox**, one hidden layer; trained with **600 samples** for the OVS-firewall model and **900 samples** for Snort and the OVS switch; test sets of **150 and 200 samples** respectively.
- **Network scale:** Overlay experiment: 12 overlay nodes / 19 underlay elements / 72 links; 9,600 training + 300 test samples. NFV experiment: 2 VNF types (OVS, Snort) in VMs; 86 features; 600/900 training samples; 150/200 test samples; traffic from a campus network of ~30k users.
- **Baselines and Metrics:** Baselines: for the NFV model, a single-feature predictor (number of flows for the firewall; number of packets for Snort) used to demonstrate that a multi-dimensional ML model is needed. Metrics: **average relative error** and **Mean Squared Error (MSE)** of predicted path delay; **relative error** (CDF) of predicted CPU consumption; model nonlinearity demonstrated via predicted-vs-measured curves.
- **Main quantitative results (exact quotes):**
 - Overlay routing: "the accuracy of the model is reasonably high, with **a relative error of roughly 1% when using 3,000 training samples**. This error is computed as the average relative error of the delay of all paths in each of the 300 samples of the test set."
 - NFV: "Fig. 6 shows a CDF of the relative error of the full model when trained with all the features, demonstrating that the model achieves reasonable accuracy." **No single numeric accuracy value is stated for the NFV model** in the text; the result is shown only as a CDF figure. Single-feature prediction is shown to be "not accurate and non-linear, which motivates the use of ML."
- **Main limitations:** As stated, the authors list four challenges: (1) **New ML mechanisms** — networking needs graph-aware ML; existing graph ML is only "preliminary attempts"; (2) **Non-deterministic networks** — ML models lack the guarantees and interpretability of analytical models, and it is an open research question what constitutes a representative training set; (3) **New skill set and mindset** — the field requires AI expertise; (4) **Standardized datasets** — the community needs public network datasets (the authors publish theirs). Methodological (our assessment): the experiments validate only the *learning* stage, not the full Sense→Learn→Act closed loop; no controller actuation was demonstrated end-to-end; no comparison against classical (non-ML) models for CPU/delay estimation; scale is small (12-node overlay; 2 VNF types); no SLA or slicing machinery.
- **Research gap this suggests:** KDN asserts an ML-driven Knowledge Plane but demonstrates only the model-learning half. The unbuilt half — trustworthy closed-loop actuation, guarantees/verification of ML decisions on non-deterministic networks, representative training sets, and standardized datasets — is exactly the gap that later AI-native orchestration (ZSM closed loops, XAI for O-RAN, LLM-based orchestrators) attempts to close.
- **Best URL actually retrieved, and whether full text or abstract:** https://arxiv.org/pdf/1606.06222v2 — **FULL TEXT** (8 pages). Published version: https://doi.org/10.1145/3138808.3138810

## Paper 6

### Orchestrating Virtualized Network Functions
- **Authors / Year / Venue / DOI / peer-reviewed or preprint:** Journal version: Md. Faizul Bari, Shihabur Rahman Chowdhury, Reaz Ahmed, Raouf Boutaba, **Otto Carlos Muniz Bandeira Duarte**. **IEEE Transactions on Network and Service Management (TNSM), vol. 13, no. 4, pp. 725-739, December 2016.** DOI **10.1109/TNSM.2016.2569020**. Peer-reviewed journal article. Conference precursor: "On orchestrating virtual network functions," CNSM 2015, pp. 50-56, DOI 10.1109/CNSM.2015.7367338.
 **ACCESS CAVEAT — IMPORTANT:** The TNSM version of record is paywalled and was **not** retrieved. The full text read is the author preprint **arXiv:1503.06377v2, titled "On Orchestrating Virtual Network Functions in NFV" (25 March 2015)**, which has **four** authors (Bari, Chowdhury, Ahmed, Boutaba — Duarte is absent) and carries no `journal_ref` in the arXiv record. All content below is from **that preprint**. The TNSM version adds a fifth author and may differ in framing and in some numbers; the quantitative claims below should be re-verified against the TNSM version of record before being cited as TNSM results.
- **Type classification:** **(B) an optimization algorithm that could be used by an orchestrator.** The contribution is a formal VNF Orchestration Problem (VNF-OP) with an ILP/CPLEX optimal formulation plus a dynamic-programming heuristic that decides *how many* VNFs to deploy and *where* to place them and how to route traffic — i.e., the placement/assignment engine an orchestrator would call, not a lifecycle-managing orchestrator.
- **Architecture:** Not a layered control architecture; it is a mathematical optimization pipeline with two solvers over one problem. Components: (i) a **physical network model** (servers, switches, links) and a **traffic request model** (traffic batch + an ordered middlebox/VNF sequence per traffic flow); (ii) **physical network transformation** — "We transform the physical network to generate an augmented pseudo-network that reduces the complexity involved" (a multi-stage graph in which each stage represents one VNF type in the required sequence, and nodes within a stage are candidate server locations); (iii) an **ILP formulation (Section III-B)** with constraints for VNF capacities, server CPU, link capacity, and flow conservation; (iv) a **heuristic** using a multi-stage graph + dynamic programming to find a near-optimal solution for larger instances.
- **Control plane / management plane location:** NOT REPORTED as a named standard. The paper is pre-MANO-standardization in orientation — it does not reference ETSI NFV MANO, OSM, ONAP, ZSM, O-RAN, or 3GPP. It defines its own "VNF Orchestration Problem" terminology and refers to an orchestrator only conceptually.
- **Inputs and Outputs:** INPUTS: physical network topology (nodes, links, link capacities); server attributes (CPU cores, idle/peak energy); a traffic batch (e.g., "about 132 requests per batch"), each request with source, destination, bandwidth demand and a required ordered **3-length middlebox sequence**; VNF attributes (CPU required and processing capacity per function type: Firewall 4 CPU / 900 Mbps, Proxy 4 / 900 Mbps, Nat 2 / 900 Mbps, IDS 8 / 600 Mbps); cost data (energy price, transit cost, SLO violation penalty). OUTPUTS: the **number of VNF instances** to instantiate, their **placement** on servers, the **routing** of each traffic flow through its VNF sequence, and the resulting OPEX and resource utilization.
- **Managed resources:** Compute (server CPU cores), energy/power (server idle 80.5 W, peak 2735 W; hardware middlebox idle 1100 W, peak 1700 W, processing capacity 40 Gbps), network bandwidth/link capacity, VNF instances and their processing capacity, hardware middleboxes (as the comparison baseline). Storage and radio: not modelled.
- **Network domains:** Data-centre and ISP/enterprise wide-area network. Evaluated on an ISP research network (Internet2), a university data-center network, and an autonomous-system topology. Single administrative domain; RAN, transport-optical, edge and 5G core: NOT REPORTED.
- **Interfaces/APIs:** NOT REPORTED (no NETCONF/YANG, REST, TMF, A1/E2/O1/O2, or Kubernetes API). Objective: minimize **OPEX** = energy cost + transit cost + SLO-violation penalty (deployment cost excluded from the VNF-vs-hardware comparison "to make the comparison fair").
- **ML/DRL/LLM/agent usage:** **NONE.** No machine learning, no reinforcement learning, no LLM, no agents. This is a purely model-based mathematical optimization contribution.

It is a cost penalty rather than a contractual SLA-enforcement mechanism. The paper notes "The SLO violation penalty is not shown as it is zero for all time-instances," meaning the solver never violated the latency SLO in these experiments. Explicit per-slice SLA objects: NOT REPORTED.
- **Network slicing support:** NOT REPORTED (the term and concept are absent; the unit of service is a traffic flow with a middlebox sequence, i.e., a service function chain).
- **Kubernetes/cloud-native support:** NOT REPORTED.
- **Evaluation environment:** **Trace-driven simulation** (the authors' own simulator; no standard simulator named — not Mininet, not Containernet, not ns-3). Workload inputs: real traffic-matrix traces for Internet2; traces from a university data-center network replayed between random source-destination pairs; a synthetic time-varying traffic matrix generated with the **FNSS tool** (following a distribution from a cited source, exhibiting time-of-day effect) for the Rocketfuel topology. Solver runs on a machine with **10 × 16-Core 2.40 GHz Intel Xeon E7-8870 CPUs and 1 TB memory**. Implementations released publicly. VNF technology referenced: ClickOS.
- **Network scale:** Three real-world topologies: **Internet2 research network (12 nodes, 15 links)**; **university data-center network (23 nodes, 42 links)** (Table II lists 23 nodes / 43 links); **Autonomous System AS-3967 from the Rocketfuel topology dataset (79 nodes, 147 links)**. Traffic: "about 132 requests per batch" over a period of 10,000 minutes; a 3-length middlebox sequence per traffic; 4 VNF types modelled (Firewall, Proxy, Nat, IDS); 16 CPU cores per server.
- **Baselines and Metrics:** BASELINES: (i) **hardware middleboxes peak-provisioned** via a separate CPLEX program (the VNF-vs-hardware comparison); (ii) **CPLEX ILP optimal solution** (the heuristic's reference). METRICS: **OPEX** (energy + transit cost, and their ratio); **execution time** to find placement for a traffic batch; **system utilization** (fraction of used CPU per server) and **number of active servers**; **topological properties** — percentage of middleboxes placed within *k* hops of ingress/egress switches, and **path stretch** (ratio of obtained path length to shortest path length).
- **Main quantitative results (exact quotes):**
 - Abstract: "Trace driven simulations on real-world network topologies demonstrate that the **heuristic can provide solutions that are within 1.3 times of the optimal solution**. Our experiments suggest that a VNF based approach can provide **more than 4× reduction in the operational cost** of a network."
 - VNFs vs hardware middleboxes: "The bottom part of Fig. 3(a) shows that **VNFs provide more than 4× reduction in OPEX**." Conclusion: "network OPEX **can be reduced by a factor of 4** over hardware middleboxes through proper VNF orchestration." The reduction in energy cost is much higher than that of transit cost.
 - Heuristic vs CPLEX cost ratio: Internet2 — "the heuristic finds solutions that are **within 1.1 times of the optimal solution**… the total cost ratio stays almost the same (**varying between 1 and 1.1**)." Data-center network — "the cost ratio is also very close to 1 and **varies between 1.1 and 1.3**."
 - Execution time (Table II, "Average Execution Time"): Internet2 (12 nodes, 15 links) — **CPLEX 34.99 s vs Heuristic 0.535 s**; Data Center (23 nodes, 43 links) — **CPLEX 1595.12 s vs Heuristic 0.442 s**; AS-3967 (79 nodes, 147 links) — **CPLEX ∞ (did not finish) vs Heuristic 2.54 s**. Conclusion: "the heuristic produces solutions that are within 1.3 times of the optimal solution, yet the **execution-time is about 65 to 3500 times faster** than that of the CPLEX solution."
 - Topology: "more than **90% middleboxes are deployed within 5 hops**" of the ingress switch.
- **Main limitations:** As stated by the authors (future work): extend the model to support **both hardware and software middleboxes in the same network**; introduce **failure-resilience** by deploying backup VNFs; enhance the physical-network transformation to further reduce the solution space and speed up the optimal solver. Methodological (our assessment, from the preprint read): (a) **CPLEX cannot scale** — it failed to return a solution (∞) on the 79-node topology, so the "optimal" reference and the 1.3× bound are unverifiable at that scale; (b) no ML/learning — every re-optimization is a fresh NP-hard solve with no warm-start or prediction, so the approach cannot respond at short timescales; (c) cost parameters are inferred from public data sheets and manufacturer data rather than measured on an operational network; (d) the SLO violation penalty is zero throughout, so latency-SLA behaviour is not actually exercised; (e) no real deployment/testbed — all results are trace-driven simulation.
- **Research gap this suggests:** The paper shows that exact VNF placement optimization does not scale and that heuristics trade ~1.1–1.3× cost optimality for orders-of-magnitude speed. The gap is a *learning-based or predictive* placement/tracking layer that (i) avoids re-solving NP-hard problems at every time-instance, (ii) predicts traffic/VNF behaviour (the very thing KDN, paper 5, proposes to learn), and (iii) is embedded in a real MANO closed loop rather than an offline solver — i.e., the AI-native orchestration agenda.

## Paper 10

### An Autonomous Network Orchestration Framework Integrating Large Language Models with Continual Reinforcement Learning
- **Authors / Year / Venue / DOI / peer-reviewed or preprint:** Masoud Shokrnezhad (University of Oulu, Finland), Tarik Taleb (Ruhr University Bochum, Germany). **IEEE Communications Magazine, vol. 63, no. 8, pp. 78-84, August 2025.** DOI **10.1109/MCOM.001.2400526**. Peer-reviewed magazine article. The full text read is the accepted-version preprint **arXiv:2502.16198v1 (22 February 2025)**, which carries the IEEE copyright notice "The DOI will be supplied as soon as it becomes available" — i.e., the arXiv PDF is the IEEE-accepted manuscript.
- **Type classification:** **(F) an LLM-based orchestrator.** The paper's named contribution is the **Autonomous Reinforcement Coordination (ARC)** framework, a two-tier orchestrator that explicitly allocates network resources — LLMs perform high-level planning/sequencing and RL agents perform low-level action execution — implemented and evaluated as a closed-loop control system around the infrastructure.
- **Architecture:** ARC has four components plus a closed-loop workflow:
 - **Static Knowledge Base (SKB)** — vector-structured database holding **Service Specifications** (each service as a directed graph of functional blocks with semantic QoE and capacity requirements), **Objective Profiles** (three system-level objectives: Minimizing Cost = normalized sum of energy consumption and monetary price; Maximizing Quality; Load Balancing — exactly one active at a time, jointly optimized with maximizing the number of supported users), and **Action Profiles** (all allocation decision types: which computing resource hosts each functional block, computing and storage capacity to allocate, and routing path selection).
 - **Dynamic Knowledge Base (DKB)** — vector store of **State History** (windowed state records, window size adaptively adjusted e.g. by ε-greedy until performance degrades) and **Reasoning Exemplars** as **[input, CoT, output] triples**, initialized "by combining the optimal allocations of solving the problem for different state histories and objectives using mathematical optimization solvers along with random and greedy action selections."
 - **Retrieval-Augmented Generator (RAG)** — the coordinating component performing, each time slot: **Objective Tracking** (LLM generates a step-back question from a strategist's command, vectorizes it, cosine-matches against SKB objective vectors, activates the most similar objective), **QoE Evaluation** (LLM scores similarity between user feedback and the stored semantic QoE requirements against a predefined threshold), **State Indexing** (encodes infrastructure+user state into DKB vectors; if QoE is met the allocation is reflected in the state, otherwise resources are discarded and the user is marked as actively seeking resources), **Reward Calculation** (zero reward if QoE unmet, else reward reflecting contribution to the active objective; published as an "update prompt"), **Experience Augmentation** (updates [input, CoT, output] exemplars), and **Allocation Prompt Generation** (builds the prompt with the highest-similarity exemplars attached).
 - **Hierarchical Action Planner (HAP)** — **User Sequencer** (an LLM derives the optimal ordering of users such that greedy per-user allocation yields the optimal outcome; the paper proves such a sequence exists and notes sequences must be produced "in one shot" because "LLMs are more adept at generating entire sequences than intermittently pausing and re-initiating the token sampling process") and **Action Executioner** (a set of specialized **RL agents, one per action** — the Mixture of Experts concept; a masked state history restricts each agent to the feasible solution space for that user/service/objective; decisions applied in sequence order, with parallel or serial execution allowed within a single user depending on the action profile).
 - **Control loop:** "This two-tier orchestrator is integrated into the core of a closed-loop control system through a RAG mechanism, where the state is periodically gathered, resource allocation decisions are made for users as actions, implemented within the infrastructure, and subsequent rewards are assessed and integrated into the system." The policy is redefined as the combination of a **sequencer policy** and an **execution policy**.
- **Control plane / management plane location:** **NOT REPORTED.** No ETSI NFV MANO, ETSI ZSM, ONAP, OSM, O-RAN SMO, 3GPP or Kubernetes control plane is named. The framework is positioned as an orchestration layer for a Space-Air-Ground Integrated Network (SAGIN) with an abstract "infrastructure"; the mapping onto standardized management planes is left open (the discussion of "integration with existing network architectures" is qualitative: the modular design "promotes the integration of various systems with different types of resources, enhancing practicality in aligning with existing network architectures").
- **Inputs and Outputs:** INPUTS: strategist textual commands (to change the active system-level objective); user service requests (which service, which network node connects the user); user feedback in semantic QoE space; monitored infrastructure state (per-[user, service, resource] triplet quality and quantity metrics); service specifications (functional-block DAGs, semantic QoE requirements, capacity requirements); action profiles; state history; reward signals. OUTPUTS: an ordered sequence of users; for each user, concrete allocation decisions — the computing resource (node) hosting each functional block, computing capacity, storage capacity, routing paths (next hop, channel assignment, transmit power in the air/space layers to satisfy data rate requirements; next hop and link bandwidth in the ground layer); the updated active objective; updated reasoning exemplars and RL agent weights.
- **Managed resources:** **Compute** (per-node 10-100 MIPS; service functional blocks require 2-5 MIPS uniformly), **storage capacity**, **network links** (bandwidth and latency), **radio-channel assignment** and **transmit power** (space/air layers), **network paths/routes**. The three SAGIN layers' nodes "are equipped with computing resources, collectively forming a distributed cloud." Explicit spectrum-resource-block handling: not separately modelled (channel assignment is the closest construct).
- **Network domains:** **Multi-domain by construction.** SAGIN with three layers: (1) **ground** — cellular and satellite ground stations interconnected by network nodes over fiber and cable links (operating at/near Earth's surface); (2) **air** — high-altitude platform stations and UAVs in the stratosphere/lower atmosphere supporting cellular and satellite interfaces; (3) **space** — satellite constellations in GEO, LEO, MEO and HEO orbits. All three layers host computing, forming a distributed cloud. This is terrestrial + non-terrestrial multi-domain orchestration.
- **Interfaces/APIs:** **NOT REPORTED.** No A1, E2, O1, O2, REST, NETCONF/YANG, TMF Open API, CAMARA or Kubernetes API is named. Interfaces are internal: LLM **prompts** ("update prompt", "allocation prompt") between RAG and HAP, and abstract "action profiles" toward the infrastructure.
- **Optimization engine:** A hybrid of three engines: (1) **LLM reasoning** for the outer-tier sequencing problem (with Chain-of-Thought few-shot prompting); (2) **Double Dueling Deep Q-Learning (D3QL)** for the inner-tier per-action agents; (3) **mathematical optimization solvers** used offline to bootstrap the initial reasoning exemplars with optimal allocations. The problem is characterized as **NP-hard** with an uncaclulable reward (QoE-based), unknown transition dynamics, and combinatorial growth.
- **ML/DRL/LLM/agent usage (exact):** **LLM: LLaMA 3.1-8B, used without fine-tuning.** Techniques: **Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)**; **Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning** via **few-shot learning** ("reward-based few-shot learning") with **contrastive learning** (RAG selects two groups of exemplars, one with the highest and one with the lowest rewards, "providing the LLM with updated instructions on what to pursue and what to avoid"); explicit hallucination mitigation ("these exemplars serve to reduce the likelihood of hallucinations by presenting the model with numerous valid responses"). **RL: Double Dueling Deep Q-Learning (D3QL)** agents with two online and target DNNs, a replay buffer, **Continual Reinforcement Learning** via **Gradient-Based Sample Selection (GDSS)** to prevent catastrophic forgetting and maximize replay-buffer diversity; batch training against the target DNN; the online DNN is periodically replaced by the target DNN. **Mixture of Experts (MoE)**: one dedicated RL agent per action. **Agents:** multiple specialized RL agents coordinated by the LLM sequencer.
- **Centralized vs distributed; single vs multi-domain; closed-loop vs open-loop; static vs dynamic:** **Hierarchical/two-tier and logically centralized** (one RAG, one HAP with a single LLM sequencer, but a *set* of distributed specialized RL agents). **Multi-domain** (ground/air/space). **Closed-loop** (per time slot: monitor → evaluate QoE → allocate → apply → reward → update exemplars and agents). **Dynamic** — explicitly designed for unknown transition dynamics (solar flares, geomagnetic storms, ionospheric fluctuation, mobile users with rapid demand shifts) with lifelong adaptation through reward-based few-shot learning and gradient-based continual learning.
- **SLA awareness:** **No formal SLA object.** The equivalent construct is **semantic-space Quality of Experience (QoE)**: services carry semantic QoE requirements, the LLM computes a similarity score between user feedback and the stored requirement against "a predefined threshold"; if the score falls below threshold the requirement is considered unmet, the resources are discarded, the user is re-marked as seeking resources and receives **reward 0**. Concrete example requirement used: for an image-processing service, "the image must be 1920x1080 to be acceptable, with dimensions depending on the allocated network link capacity."
- **Network slicing support:** **NOT REPORTED** (the term and the slice abstraction are absent). The service abstraction is a **directed graph of functional blocks with semantic QoE requirements** — i.e., a semantic service function chain rather than a network slice. Hierarchical slice aggregation is not discussed.
- **Kubernetes/cloud-native support:** **NOT REPORTED.** No container/pod/Kubernetes construct appears; deployment is described at the level of functional blocks placed on nodes.
- **Evaluation environment:** **Numerical simulations** using a bespoke **proof-of-concept simulator** (the paper says "we evaluate ARC through numerical simulations"; no named simulator, emulator, testbed, Mininet, Containernet, Kubernetes, Open5GS, OSM, ONAP, OpenAirInterface, Colosseum or 5GinFIRE is used). The LLM is **LLaMA 3.1-8B without fine-tuning**; RL agents use **D3QL**; exemplars bootstrapped with mathematical optimization solvers.
- **Network scale:** **An infrastructure of 10 nodes, half of which are non-terrestrial** (so 5 terrestrial, 5 airborne/spaceborne), the non-terrestrial nodes "follow a predefined movement pattern to maintain periodic connectivity and connect to three nearest neighbors." **10 users**, each requesting a service "involving a single functional block" whose computing capacity is uniformly selected from **2 to 5 MIPS**. Node computing capacity **10 to 100 MIPS**; link latency **1 to 10 ms**; link capacity **10 to 100 Mbps** with "the associated costs have the same numerical values as the link capacities." Time-slot count, number of services, and number of episodes: NOT REPORTED. One deliberate mid-run transition at **iteration 30,000**, "during which we modified the link latency calculation by swapping low-latency links with high-latency ones."
- **Baselines and Metrics:** BASELINES: (i) **optimal** (mathematical-optimization-solver solution used to initialize exemplars); (ii) **RU-ARC (Reward-Unaware ARC)** — "which disregards reward calculations and provides HAP with reasoning exemplars based solely on similarity, without training RL agents, which operate on pre-trained weights"; (iii) **NR-ARC (Non-Reinforcement ARC)** — "eliminating RL agents and allowing the LLM to manage low-level allocations." METRICS: **normalized cost** — "The normalized cost of each allocation is calculated to ensure that allocations with lower costs receive higher scores, and vice versa"; **reward** = "dividing the average resource cost by each action's allocated cost, thereby favoring low-cost allocations"; objective = minimize allocation cost while maximizing the number of supported users.
- **Main quantitative results (exact quotes):** The evaluation is an **ablation study presented only as two figures (Fig. 4.A and Fig. 4.B); no numerical values are stated in the text.** Exact qualitative claims: "The findings indicate that **ARC allocates resources efficiently, achieving near-optimal cost outcomes**. After a transition at iteration 30,000… **ARC exhibits a notable recovery capability, whereas RU-ARC fails to demonstrate similar resilience**." And: "Results show **NR-ARC performs worse than ARC, displaying greater fluctuations due to LLM instability**." Conclusion: ARC demonstrates "efficiency, accuracy, and adaptability, especially when compared to prior approaches that employed LLMs for resource allocation decision-making without incorporating feedback mechanisms." **No percentages, no absolute cost values, and no confidence intervals are provided.**
- **Main limitations:** As stated: this is a **proof-of-concept simulation** ("Following the demonstration of the performance of ARC through proof-of-concept simulations"); the authors list as future directions **prediction-based state indexing** (a GAN with an LSTM generator and RNN discriminator to predict future states), **autonomous service/action extension** (LLM defines unknown services as combinations of existing ones), **online LLM training** (the efficiency of the user sequencer "relies on the quality of allocation prompts generated by RAG"), and **Algorithm-of-Thoughts (AoT)** — because initial exemplars come from mathematical solvers with an unknown step-by-step process, "this may result in unpredictability," and AoT would trade optimality for determinism. Methodological (our assessment): (a) the evaluation is a **single small-scale ablation** — 10 nodes, 10 users, one functional block per service, one deliberate transition — with no comparison to any external published orchestrator or to a standard DRL/optimization baseline; (b) results are **figure-only with no numbers**, which blocks reproduction and meta-analysis; (c) the LLM used (LLaMA 3.1-8B) is comparatively small and its instability is acknowledged as causing NR-ARC's fluctuations — the corresponding risk in ARC's outer tier is not separately quantified; (d) bootstrapping exemplars with an exact solver means the method is only viable where an offline solver exists, which the paper admits is not generally the case for the target NP-hard problem; (e) no SLA, no slicing, no standardized management-plane integration, and no real testbed.
- **Research gap this suggests:** ARC is the clearest example in this set of the **LLM-for-high-level-planning + RL-for-low-level-execution** pattern, and it exposes the open problems for AI-native orchestration: hallucination control and verification of LLM decisions, adapting LLMs to non-stationary network dynamics without fine-tuning, bootstrapping without an exact solver, quantitative and reproducible benchmarking against external baselines, and grounding the framework in a standardized management plane (ETSI ZSM / O-RAN SMO) with real slice/SLA objects rather than abstract services.
- **Best URL actually retrieved, and whether full text or abstract:** https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.16198v1 — **FULL TEXT** (7-page IEEE-accepted manuscript). Published version: https://doi.org/10.1109/MCOM.001.2400526

## Paper 11

### Using Distributed Reinforcement Learning for Resource Orchestration in a Network Slicing Scenario
- **Authors / Year / Venue / DOI / peer-reviewed or preprint:** Federico Mason (University of Padova), Gianfranco Nencioni (University of Stavanger), Andrea Zanella (University of Padova). **IEEE/ACM Transactions on Networking, vol. 31, no. 1, pp. 88-102, February 2023.** DOI **10.1109/TNET.2022.3187310**. Peer-reviewed journal article. The full text read is the author preprint **arXiv:2105.07946v1 (17 May 2021)**, whose header states "©2021 IEEE. This paper is under review at IEEE Transaction on Networking" — i.e., the arXiv PDF is the **pre-review submission**, not the accepted version. Content should be treated as preprint-sourced; the published ToN version may differ.
- **Type classification:** **(B) an optimization algorithm that could be used by an orchestrator.** The paper designs a distributed DRL *resource-allocation* architecture whose "local controllers" output per-flow bandwidth/compute/memory assignments each timeslot; it contains no service lifecycle management, no descriptor/VNF onboarding, no placement of VNF instances, and no MANO integration — so it is an allocation engine an orchestrator would invoke, not an orchestrator.
- **Architecture:** A **distributed, hierarchical learning architecture**:
 - **Local controllers** — "a distributed architecture based on multiple learning units, named local controllers, that collaborate to maximize the overall system utility"; each local controller is associated with (and "has full knowledge of") a specific network element, and the number/placement of local controllers "depends on the network topology and the cardinality of Φ [the set of flows]". They are **link controllers** and **node controllers**.
 - **Per-slice-class agent set** — for each slice class σ ∈ Σ, three agent pairs: **Γ^b_σ** (bandwidth), **Γ^c_σ** (computational power), **Γ^m_σ** (memory capacity), each with an **actor** and a **critic** network.
 - **Actor–Critic learning units** — "The first is named actor and approximates the optimal policy π_θ, parameterized by θ; the latter is named critic and approximates the value function V_γ."
 - **Training manager (centralized, offline)** — "a central entity, named training manager, which collect[s] the system observations"; the A2C algorithm updates policies only at the end of a training episode, local observations are collected "in a central database, which is then used to carry out the training phase," after which "the new versions of the learning agents [are] shared within the network, and the resource allocation strategy is updated consequently." This is the paper's answer to the partial-observability problem: "each local controller has full knowledge of [its own element but] the whole system is partially observable."
 - Control loop: per-timeslot observe → act → reward; policy updates per episode (offline, "does not have any delay requirements").
- **Control plane / management plane location:** **NOT REPORTED as an implemented standard.** The paper frames the problem in the 3GPP/SDN/NFV world conceptually: 3GPP is cited for the three service classes (eMBB, URLLC, mMTC, TS 22.261) and for the 5G system architecture (TS 23.501) and policy/charging (TS 23.203); the **slice broker** is described as "the body in charge of initializing and orchestrating slices," acquired from infrastructure providers. It does **not** implement ETSI NFV MANO, ETSI ZSM, ONAP, OSM, O-RAN SMO or Kubernetes. Explicitly: no ETSI/ONAP/OSM mention occurs in the text.
- **Inputs and Outputs:** INPUTS: per-flow resource demand vectors **r_φ = [η_φ, c_φ, m_φ, δ_φ]** — throughput η [bps], computational power c [bps], memory capacity m [bits], delay δ [s] — where η, c and m change in time via an independent **Markov Model** per slice (10 states, transitions only between adjacent states); flow endpoints E_φ (base stations, edge routers of autonomous systems, or servers); the performance function F_φ(·) and delay requirement δ_φ (both static per flow); local observations at each network element. OUTPUTS: the assigned resource vector **r̂_φ(t) = [η̂_φ(t), ĉ_φ(t), m̂_φ(t), δ̂_φ(t)]** per flow per timeslot — concretely the bandwidth allocated per link per flow, and the computational and memory capacity allocated at nodes per flow.
- **Managed resources:** **Link bandwidth** (B_l, link rate capacity), **node computational capacity** (C^c_n), **node memory capacity** (C^m_n), and **delay** as the derived performance dimension. Explicitly, each flow "contend[s] for the bandwidth provided by network links, and the computational and memory resources provided by the computing facilities connected with the network nodes." Compute resources are described as "physical (e.g., optical links) and virtual (e.g., virtual baseband processing units)". Radio/spectrum: not modelled. VNFs and pods: not modelled (no VNF instances are placed).
- **Network domains:** Transport/network plus attached compute. The model's nodes are "core nodes" and "access nodes" (access nodes are where users' data enter/exit the slice); "we consider that most computing and storage resources are concentrated in the core network." A single infrastructure domain shared by multiple slice tenants, with **hierarchical slices** ("network slices are organized hierarchically, so that more flows can be compounded into an aggregate slice, possibly managed by a different tenant") that "can model the interactions among slice tenants, and with slice brokers." RAN and edge as separate domains: NOT REPORTED.
- **Interfaces/APIs:** **NOT REPORTED.** No A1, E2, O1, O2, REST, NETCONF/YANG, TMF Open API, CAMARA or Kubernetes API. Inter-entity communication is described only abstractly: "the different network elements collect local observations in a central database" and trained agents are "shared within the network"; the authors argue this control traffic "should not represent a limiting factor" because SDN/NFV already require such exchange.
- **Optimization engine:** **Advantage Actor Critic (A2C)** deep reinforcement learning, chosen because "A2C enables to consider continuous action spaces: this is not possible with traditional Reinforcement learning algorithms (e.g., Q-Learning)." Formally: critic minimizes L_c(s_t,a_t) = A(s_t,a_t)² with advantage A(s_t,a_t) = r_t + λV_γ(s_{t+1}) − V_γ(s_t); actor minimizes L_a(s_t,a_t) = −∇_θ log π_θ(s_t,a_t)A(s_t,a_t)² − κH(π_θ). Weights optimized with **Adam**; entropy weight κ encourages exploration. Also employed: **transfer learning** across topologies/loads. The paper discusses **Genetic Algorithms (GA)** as a meta-heuristic alternative but rejects them as an online engine: "meta-heuristic algorithms are based on a randomized search of the target solution and require an extremely long calculation time, which makes it unfeasible to execute them at each timeslot."
- **ML/DRL/LLM/agent usage (exact):** **Deep Reinforcement Learning — distributed multi-agent A2C.** Multiple cooperating agents (3 per slice class: bandwidth, compute, memory), each an actor–critic pair with its own DNN. **Agent architectures (Table III):** Γ^b_σ actors/critics — input size **11** (and 7 for Γ^c_σ / Γ^m_σ), **ReLU** activations, hidden sizes **12 → 6** (Γ^b) and **8 → 4** (Γ^c, Γ^m), **Linear** output size **1**. Hyperparameters: actor learning rate ζ_a = **10⁻⁵**, critic learning rate ζ_c = **10⁻⁵**, discount factor λ = **0.9**, entropy weight κ = **10⁻⁴**, reward weights {γ₀, γ₁} = **{0.1, 1}**, eMBB performance weights {α_η, α_c, α_m, α_δ} = **{0.25, 0.25, 0.25, 0.25}**. **No LLM, no agentic-LLM component** (2021 paper). Agents cooperate toward a common goal (maximizing system utility) — i.e., **fully cooperative multi-agent DRL, not competitive.**
- **Centralized vs distributed; single vs multi-domain; closed-loop vs open-loop; static vs dynamic:** **Distributed execution with centralized offline training.** The allocation decisions are made locally by distributed agent pairs (fully distributed inference), while training is centralized through the training manager's central observation database and then broadcast. **Single-domain** (one infrastructure, multiple tenants/slices within it). **Closed-loop** (per-timeslot reward feedback; policies refined continuously — "the A2C algorithm supports an online training phase and, consequently, allows the agents to continuously refine the target policy while it is being used in the real system. Therefore, the slice broker does not need to train a new system from scratch every time the network conditions change"). **Dynamic** — Markov-modulated demands that can exceed aggregate capacity, so the strategy must decide which flow to penalize.
- **SLA awareness:** **Yes.** Each flow carries a **performance function F_σ(·) ∈ [0,1] where "1 means that the SLA has been completely fulfilled"**, computed as a combination of four per-resource functions f_σ(·) over ρ ∈ {δ, η, c, m}. The eMBB class uses a **smooth concave** performance function (weighted sum of cubic polynomials) "embod[ying] the flexibility of the SLA for eMBB services"; URLLC uses a **step-like** function ("the performance of the URLLC traffic flows… goes to zero as soon as one of the requirements is not satisfied"). System utility Ω aggregates per-slice performance: R = Σ λ^t r_t, with Ω = γ₀Ω_e + γ₁Ω_u. The SLA is thus a soft, continuously-valued satisfaction score, not a contract-enforcement mechanism.
- **Network slicing support:** **Yes — this is the core scenario.** Two slice classes, **eMBB** and **URLLC**, sharing one infrastructure; hierarchical slicing where flows are compounded into aggregate slices that may be managed by different tenants; slice broker and slice tenant roles; the framework is explicitly stated to be extensible: "the slice broker can take advantage of the generality [of the model by] defining other slices with different performance functions."
- **Kubernetes/cloud-native support:** **NOT REPORTED.**
- **Evaluation environment:** **The authors' own custom simulation** (no named simulator, emulator or testbed — not Mininet, Containernet, ns-3, Kubernetes, Open5GS, OSM, ONAP, OpenAirInterface, Colosseum or 5GinFIRE). The model is a **fluid traffic model** with discretized timeslots; the DRL agents are custom neural networks in (presumably) Python/PyTorch or TensorFlow — **the framework/library is NOT REPORTED** in the retrieved preprint. Three network topologies, given as **Dumbbell (D)**, **Triangle (T)** and **Pyramid Network (P)**, plus a modified **"Pyramid+"** scenario (URLLC throughput requirement increased). Traffic modelled by a 10-state Markov Model per slice.
- **Network scale:** **Number of information flows N_Φ ∈ {2, …, 6}** in the main scenarios, with a scalability study extending the x-axis to **|Φ| = 2…9**. Capacities: **B_l = 50 Gbps** (link rate); **C^c_n = 60 Gbps and C^m_n = 60 Gb for core nodes**; **C^c_n = 20 Gbps and C^m_n = 20 Gb for access nodes**. Delays: link propagation delay τ^lp = **0.1 ms** for all links; node routing delay τ_n = **0.001 ms**. Traffic demands — **eMBB**: η 0.30-42.5 Gbps, c 50-100 Gbps, m 50-100 Gb, δ 20 ms; **URLLC**: η 2.08-10 Gbps, c 50-100 Gbps, m 50-100 Gb, δ 1 ms. Training: **N_train = {3, 5}·10⁴ episodes**, **N_slot = 50 timeslots** per episode, **T = 0.1 s** timeslot duration, **N_test = 500 testing episodes**, **N_state = 10** Markov states; transfer learning uses **N_transfer = 2·10⁴** episodes. Number of slices: 2 classes (eMBB, URLLC). Number of domains: 1.
- **Baselines and Metrics:** BASELINES: (i) **Static** allocation ("statically allocating communication and computational resources to the different slices"); (ii) **Empirical** strategy (an "efficient empirical strategy"); (iii) a **Genetic Algorithm** meta-heuristic is discussed as a static best-case but not run online; and the trained variants are compared against each other — **DRL-D** (trained in Dumbbell), **DRL-T** (trained in Triangle), **DRL-DP** and **DRL-TP** (transfer-learned into Pyramid). METRICS: **system utility Ω** (weighted aggregate of slice performances) with its expectation **E[Ω]**, median and 25th/75th percentiles; **per-resource expected utility E[Ω_ρ]** for ρ ∈ {η, c, m, δ}; **Ω > threshold** probability across test episodes; and scalability of E[Ω] versus |Φ|.
- **Main quantitative results (exact quotes):**
 - Training: "the performance of eMBB slices (Ω_e) increases slowly, but smoothly during the training phase, **ranging from 0.6 to 0.8 in 50% of the cases**. Conversely, the performance of URLLC slices (Ω_u) remains very low at the beginning of the training phase and suddenly increases after a certain number [of episodes]."
 - Dumbbell: "**DRL-D and DRL-T ensure that Ω > 0.45 in almost 50% of the test episodes, with a 10% gain over the empirical algorithm.**"
 - Triangle/Pyramid: "Using the empirical algorithm, **50% of the test episodes experience Ω > 0.5** while, with the DRL strategies, **this threshold is raised to 0.65 and 0.7**, respectively."
 - Cross-topology generalization: "DRL-D and DRL-T achieve similar results in all the testing scenarios, including the Pyramid Network, which is a different environment from those seen during the training. This means that our architecture **can suit multiple network topologies without the need of an additional learning phase**."
 - Transfer learning: "using the **DRL-DP strategy, the expectation of Ω_c increases by more than 5%**." And "DRL-DP and DRL-TP outperform DRL-D and DRL-T, respectively, despite the total number of training episodes is the same."
 - Static baseline: "the static strategy always yield[s] to the worst performance: this is because it does not handle the variability of service demands."
 - Pyramid+ (harder URLLC): "the empirical strategy **slightly outperforms DRL-T for what concerns the throughput and delay requirements**. Nevertheless, DRL-T still provides good results."
- **Main limitations:** As stated: (1) the framework assumes **static routes** for information flows (routes are assigned at episode start and not optimized); (2) it is validated only by **simulation**; (3) the authors state they "will investigate **call-admission control** strategies to avoid system overloading" in future work — i.e., the framework has no admission control, so overload is handled only by penalizing flows; (4) "we expect that a more specific training can further improve the behavior of the local controllers." Methodological (our assessment): (a) the results are **simulation-only with small topologies** (2-6 flows) and hand-set arbitrary link/node parameters ("Although arbitrary, these values are well-aligned with the features of modern network elements"); (b) the **published version was not retrieved** — all numbers are from the **pre-review 2021 preprint** and may have changed in the 2023 ToN version; (c) the centralized training via a central observation database is a scalability and single-point-of-failure concern that the paper acknowledges only qualitatively; (d) in the harder Pyramid+ scenario the DRL approach is actually **beaten by the simple empirical heuristic** on throughput and delay — the paper reports this but does not fully explain it; (e) no standardized management-plane mapping, no SLA contract enforcement, no VNF placement, no real traffic.
- **Research gap this suggests:** The paper demonstrates that distributed DRL can beat static and empirical allocation and can transfer across topologies, but it stops at resource *allocation* over a fixed routing and a single domain with a centralized offline trainer. The gap: an orchestration layer that (i) jointly optimizes routing/placement and allocation rather than assuming static routes, (ii) includes admission control, (iii) decentralizes or federates training to avoid a central observation database, (iv) enforces hard SLA contracts rather than soft satisfaction scores, and (v) closes the loop across RAN/transport/core domains — the multi-domain, multi-agent DRL direction later identified as open by the COMST 2024 resource-management survey (paper 9).

Published version: https://doi.org/10.1109/TNET.2022.3187310 (paywalled, not retrieved).

- **Type classification:** **(F) an LLM-based orchestrator.** It is a working, evaluated system that "orchestrates both LLM and non-LLM agents" across the complete intent lifecycle — translation, activation and closed-loop assurance — deployed against two industry-grade SDN controllers, with the LLM as the central decision component rather than an offline helper.
- **Architecture:** A **three-stage intent lifecycle pipeline**, each stage a distinct subsystem:
 1. **Intent Translation** — a prompt plus a **context-example store** (few-shot input/output pairs) feeds an LLM that emits JSON-structured configuration for the target controller. Output passes to a **validator** checking syntax and controller-required tags; on failure the LLM is automatically re-prompted with the flow rule, the validator feedback and a new guiding prompt — a **re-prompting loop repeating until a valid configuration is obtained** (Algorithm 1).
 2. **Intent Activation** — a **conflict-detection module** retrieves existing configurations from the target controller and checks the new rule against them (Algorithm 2), handling **Overlap, Implication/Shadowing and Correlation** conflict classes by reasoning about match spaces and actions. A **resolver** attempts resolution; if resolution fails the intent is **not** activated and the user is notified. On success the rule is installed via the controller's southbound API and then **verified by inspecting operational stats**.
 3. **Intent Assurance (closed loop)** — **non-LLM agents** collect intent-drift and device-state information and pass it to the LLM, which generates **corrective actions** executed by code modules, continuing "in a closed-loop fashion" (Algorithm 3), with **ranked corrective actions** and a set of common remediation routines. Assurance covers both **static** (config datastore vs operational datastore) and **dynamic** (real-time flow-rule forwarding behaviour) verification.
 **Agent decomposition:** explicitly a **hybrid LLM / non-LLM agent architecture** — the LLM does translation, conflict reasoning and corrective-action generation; deterministic code modules do retrieval, validation, installation, statistics collection and action execution. A three-stage **ranked-LLM** strategy selects among candidate models inside translation.
- **Control plane / management plane location:** The **SDN control plane**, instantiated on **OpenDaylight (ODL)** and **ONOS**. ODL was deployed on **Karaf 0.8.4**; **ONOS version 2.0.0**. **No ETSI NFV MANO, ETSI ZSM, ONAP, OSM, O-RAN SMO or 3GPP management-plane mapping is claimed or implemented** — it is an SDN controller-plane system, not an NFV/SMO orchestrator.
- **Inputs and Outputs:** INPUTS: high-level **natural-language intents** (three categories: **Forwarding, Security, QoS**); **few-shot context examples** (context size swept over 0/1/3/…/9, with **3** used for final end-to-end runs); **existing flow rules** from the live controller's config datastore; for assurance, **operational datastore contents and flow statistics**, plus validation feedback on syntax errors. OUTPUTS: **controller-specific JSON flow rules** (ODL-style flow entries / ONOS-style flow rules) conforming to the controller's YANG-derived schema; **conflict verdicts** and resolved or rejected intents; **installed and verified flow rules**; **corrective actions** during assurance; user notification when an intent cannot be activated. Example intents: *"Deny packets originating from 10.0.0.1 destined for 10.0.0.4 using switch 1"* and *"Do slicing at node 3 and ensure TCP packets destined for port 80, addressed to 10.0.0.3, are forwarded via port 2 using queue 0."*

Compute, memory, VNFs, pods, radio and spectrum are not managed.
- **Network domains:** **Transport / data-center SDN — single administrative domain.** No RAN, no 5G core, no multi-domain or inter-operator orchestration.
- **Interfaces/APIs:** **RESTCONF** (southbound API installing rules into **ODL**) and **ONOS `FlowRuleService`** (installing rules into ONOS): *"the system proceeds to install the rule via the southbound API—RESTCONF for ODL or FlowRuleService for ONOS."* Underlying data plane is **OpenFlow**. ODL's config/operational **data stores** are read for conflict detection and assurance; ODL **DLUX** and the ONOS **web GUI** are named as the manual alternatives being replaced. **No A1, E2, O1, O2, NETCONF/YANG as a northbound contract, TMF Open API, CAMARA or Kubernetes API** — YANG appears only as the schema model the JSON must conform to.
- **Optimization engine:** **None.** No mathematical optimizer, solver or objective function. The "engine" is the LLM plus deterministic validation/conflict/assurance algorithms (Algorithms 1-3). This is a rule- and LLM-driven pipeline, not an optimization formulation — a meaningful contrast with Papers 12, 13 and 16b.
- **ML/DRL/LLM/agent usage:** **33 open-source LLMs** benchmarked (IBNBench), with **32** producing meaningful results on the Formal-specification dataset (**Deepseek-coder-v2:16b failed**, lacking "K-shift" support required for long prompts) and **28** included in FlowConflict-ODL results after excluding 5 (Phi, Orca-mini, Qwen, tinyLlama, Deepseek-coder-V2). Models named include **QwQ:32b, QwQ-fusion:32b, QwQ-abliterated:32b, Command-r, Codestral:22b, Codellama:7b/70b, Mistral:7b, Mistral-nemo:12b, Llama2:70b, Llama3.3:70b, Llama3.1:8b, Gemma2:27B, Marco-o1:7b, Deepseek-coder:1.3b, Starcoder:3b, Starcoder2:3b, Codegemma:7b, Openchat:7b, Phi3:8b, Wizardlm2:7b, Yi:6b, TinyLlama:1.1b, Orca-mini:3b, Qwen2.5-7b**. **All models open-source** (deliberate, to avoid closed-source dependencies), downloaded via **Ollama**, orchestrated with **LangChain**. **No fine-tuning** — pretrained state only; adaptation is by **few-shot in-context learning** and prompt design. Hyperparameters: intent translation **temperature 0.6, top-p 0.3**; conflict detection **temperature 0.3, top-p 0.5**. **Selected production models:** for **ODL**, **QwQ:32b** for both translation and conflict detection; for **ONOS**, **Codestral:22b** for translation and **QwQ:32b** for conflict detection; context examples **3** on both controllers.
- **Centralized vs distributed; single vs multi-domain; closed-loop vs open-loop; static vs dynamic:** **Centralized** — a single logical orchestration pipeline driving one logically-centralized SDN controller per deployment (evaluated separately on ODL and ONOS). **Single-domain.** **Closed-loop** — explicitly; assurance runs continuously in the background, comparing intent against observed state and issuing corrective actions. **Dynamic** — adapts at run time via validator-driven re-prompting, conflict-aware resolution and drift-triggered remediation.
- **SLA awareness:** **Partial/indirect.** No SLA object, contract or violation lifecycle is defined. However **QoS is one of the three first-class intent categories**, with explicit queue-and-bandwidth intents (examples reference URLLC slices, latency below 5 ms and 50 Mbps minimum bandwidth as IBN motivation), and the **assurance loop verifies intent conformance continuously** and triggers corrective action on drift. Report as: **yes, via QoS intents + assurance; no formal SLA lifecycle.**
- **Network slicing support:** **Limited / illustrative only.** Slicing appears as an *example intent* ("Do slicing at node 3 and ensure TCP packets destined for port 80 … forwarded via port 2 using queue 0") and the URLLC slice is introductory motivation, but there is **no slice lifecycle management, no slice isolation mechanism, no NSD/NST composition and no slice-level resource model**. Queue-based QoS differentiation is the nearest implemented analogue; the paper's scope statement limits it to three foundational intent categories.
- **Kubernetes/cloud-native support:** **No** — not mentioned or used anywhere.
- **Evaluation environment:** **Mininet** — a **diamond-shaped topology with four switches (s1-s4) and four hosts (h1-h4)**; h1/h2 attach to s1, h3/h4 to s4. Two distinct s1→s4 paths: via **s2 with lower-bandwidth links (1 Mbps)** and via **s3 with higher-bandwidth links (10 Mbps)**. To exercise QoS, **traffic queues were created on s3 at 6 Mbps (Queue 0) and 4 Mbps (Queue 1)**. The identical topology was deployed separately for **ODL** and **ONOS**. Controllers: **OpenDaylight on Karaf 0.8.4**; **ONOS 2.0.0**. LLM runtime: **Ollama** for model serving, **LangChain** for LLM operation management, Python implementation. Hardware: **AMD Ryzen Threadripper PRO 5995WX 64-core CPU, 500 GB RAM, NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPU with 48 GB VRAM**. Not used: Containernet, Kubernetes, Open5GS, OSM, ONAP, OpenAirInterface, Colosseum, 5GinFIRE.
- **Network scale:** **4 OpenFlow switches, 4 hosts**, one controller per run, 2 paths with 1 Mbps / 10 Mbps links, 2 queues on s3 at 6 Mbps / 4 Mbps. Dataset scale: **Formal specification dataset 1500 sample pairs**; **NFV configuration dataset 120 pairs**; **Intent2Flow-ODL 52 pairs**; **Intent2Flow-ONOS 50 pairs**; **FlowConflict-ODL 60 pairs**; **FlowConflict-ONOS 74 pairs**. Conflict-detection evaluation used **50 pairs** from FlowConflict-ODL (**4 conflicting, 46 non-conflicting**) and **62 pairs** from FlowConflict-ONOS. End-to-end realisation used **10 natural-language intents**. **33 LLMs** benchmarked.
- **Baselines and Metrics:** BASELINES: **model-versus-model across 33 open-source LLMs** on IBNBench and two existing datasets, with **context-example count (Ctx 0/1/3/…/9)** as the controlled factor; a qualitative capability comparison against **14 prior IBN works** in Table 1; and **three 70-billion-parameter LLMs** (Llama3.3:70b, Llama2:70b, Codellama:70b) on Intent2Flow-ONOS to test the scaling hypothesis. **There is no non-LLM or rule-based system implemented as a quantitative baseline.** METRICS: intent-translation **accuracy (%)** and **average run time (s)**; conflict-detection **TP, FP, FN, TN, Accuracy, Precision, Recall, F1-Score, FPR** and **average time**; end-to-end **duration in seconds** from intent translation to confirmed rule activation (assurance deliberately excluded from timing because it runs continuously); token/model resource metrics.
- **Main quantitative results (exact quotes):**
 - Formal specification dataset: *"Large models like QwQ-fusion and Command-r achieve high accuracy (≥ 99%) on the Formal specification dataset, while mid-sized models such as Codellama:7b and Mistral:7b perform strongly (up to 95%) with lower latency, especially when supported by in-context examples."*
 - In-context learning: *"substantial accuracy gains (up to 30%) with more context examples, highlighting the strong impact of in-context learning when paired with well-designed prompts."*
 - **Model size does not determine accuracy:** *"Notably, Llama3.3:70b achieved an 88% accuracy with zero-shot prompts, surpassing both Llama2:70b and Codellama:70b. We could not collect results for larger context examples for Llama 3.3 due to hardware memory constraints."* And: *"Codellama:70b and Llama2:70b showed only modest accuracy gains despite increasing context, and their overall performance lagged behind smaller, code-specialized models like Codestral:22b."*
 - Controller asymmetry: *"LLMs generally perform better on ONOS than on ODL in zero-shot (Ctx 0) scenarios… However, as more context is provided (Ctx 1 and Ctx 3), this advantage shifts, and a greater number of models achieve higher accuracy on ODL."*
 - Small models fail: *"Smaller models (e.g., TinyLlama:1.1b, Orca-mini:3b) struggled to generalize, often failing to produce syntactically valid outputs due to parameter and context limitations."*
 - Saturation: *"diminishing gains for some large models (e.g., QwQ-fusion) with more context imply a saturation effect, reinforcing the importance of task-specialized tuning over raw parameter count."*
 - Conflict detection (FlowConflict-ODL, 50 pairs, 4 conflicting / 46 non-conflicting): *"Models such as QwQ-abliterated:32b, QwQ-fusion:32b demonstrated exemplary performance, achieving near-perfect results (TP = 4, FP around 7, TN around 39) with minimal FP or FN. QwQ fell slightly behind by achieving TP = 3."*
 - **End-to-end realisation, ODL (Table 22, QwQ:32b translation + QwQ:32b conflict detection, 10 intents):** E2E times = **31.34, 29.75, 24.02, 25.47, 23.07, 35.10, 33.70, 20.10, 26.50, 33.60 s** → range ≈ **20.10-35.10 s**. Intent mix: 2 Security, 4 QoS, 4 Forwarding.
 - **End-to-end realisation, ONOS (Table 23, Codestral:22b translation + QwQ:32b detection, 10 intents):** E2E times = **30.34, 25.13, 21.02, 28.56, 22.07, 19.58, 31.50, 17.90, 23.21, 29.20 s** → range ≈ **17.90-31.50 s**.
 - Every intent in both controllers was translated, conflict-checked, installed and operationally verified, with the user interacting only at the intent level — *"a consistent and adaptive end-to-end IBN realization."*
- **Main limitations:** As stated by the authors (Section VII): benchmarks cover only *"representative natural language intents but do not encompass all possible configuration types or complex multi-intent scenarios"*; LLMs were evaluated *"in their pretrained state, without task-specific fine-tuning"*; and *"while NetIntent detects performance drift of intents, it does not address semantic drift — the presence of intents that are not specified by the user but are present in the network."* Future work: fine-tuned and smaller task-optimised models, quantisation/pruning/knowledge distillation for resource-constrained deployment, custom pretraining, more efficient prompt designs to cut translation errors and conflict-detection FP/FN, and better LLM explanations. Methodological: (1) **evaluation scale is tiny** — 4 switches, 4 hosts, one Mininet topology, 10 intents end-to-end, against the paper's own framing of "thousands of operational intents" in real networks; (2) **latency is operationally prohibitive** — 20-35 s end-to-end, orders of magnitude from near-real-time operations, and the paper reports but does not reconcile this with any operational timescale; (3) **no quantitative non-LLM baseline** — the comparison is LLM-vs-LLM, with prior rule-based/ML systems compared only qualitatively, so the claimed advantages over incumbents are unmeasured; (4) **conflict-detection evaluation is severely class-imbalanced** — 4 positives out of 50, accuracy is a weak metric at that prevalence, and FP counts of "around 7" against 46 negatives indicate non-trivial false alarms that the "near-perfect" framing understates; five models were excluded outright; (5) **no real traffic or real devices** — assurance validated against controller-internal state in Mininet; (6) **no fine-tuning and no prompt-injection/adversarial robustness evaluation**, despite the LLM consuming operator text and driving configuration changes; (7) no SLA contract, no cost/latency budget analysis, no multi-domain or multi-vendor evaluation.
- **Research gap this suggests:** (i) **Latency is the binding constraint on LLM-native orchestration and it is unaddressed** — a 20-35 s closed loop is incompatible with dynamic SLA-management cadence; gap: **hierarchical/distilled translation** (small task-specific model for the common case, LLM fallback for hard intents), caching/compilation of repeated intent patterns, and latency-aware agent architectures decoupling fast validation from slow generation. (ii) The **negative result on model scale is a reusable finding** — parameter count does not predict IBN translation quality; training-data alignment and prompt/schema design do — arguing for **task-specialised fine-tuned models with published benchmarks**. (iii) **Assurance is the weakest link across the LLM-IBN literature** — NetIntent implements a closed assurance loop but excludes it from timing, does not handle semantic drift, and does not evaluate it against real traffic; gap: **long-horizon, quantified intent-assurance evaluation** (drift-detection latency, false-remediation rate, convergence under realistic traffic) plus **semantic-drift detection**. (iv) **Conflict detection needs class-balanced benchmarks and calibrated thresholds** — balanced, adversarially constructed flow-conflict corpora with precision-recall curves rather than single-point accuracy. (v) **Nothing here is assessed for safety or security** — an LLM translating natural language directly into network configuration is an obvious injection target; gap: **adversarial robustness and safety-guardrail evaluation for intent translation**. (vi) **Scope is one SDN domain** — no slice lifecycle, no RAN, no multi-domain, so how an SDN-scoped LLM orchestrator would federate with standards-based intent handlers is open.

## Paper 16b

### Chat-Driven Optimal Management for Virtual Network Services
- **Authors / Year / Venue / DOI / peer-reviewed or preprint:** Yuya Miyaoka, Masaki Inoue (Department of Applied Physics and Physico-Informatics, Keio University, Japan); Kengo Urata (Network Service Laboratories, NTT, Inc., Tokyo); Shigeaki Harada (IOWN Product Design Center, NTT, Inc., Tokyo). **2026** for the journal version (arXiv v1 posted 31 Dec 2025). **IEEE Transactions on Network and Service Management (TNSM).** DOI **10.1109/TNSM.2026.3726950**. arXiv:**2512.24614**. **PEER-REVIEWED.** The paper states it *"is an extended version of our previous conference paper"* — **"Chat-Driven Interface for Virtual Network Reallocation," ICC 2025 - IEEE International Conference on Communications, DOI 10.1109/ICC52391.2025.11160902.**
- **Type classification:** **(F) an LLM-based orchestrator.** It is a complete **chat-driven network management framework** closing a loop from natural-language user chat through intent interpretation to executed VM-placement and routing reconfiguration. **Important nuance:** the **LLM performs only intent extraction** (mapping chat to a discrete update marker Δθ); the actual allocation decisions are made by a conventional **ILP** optimizer. So the LLM-based orchestrator here **wraps a (B)-type optimization engine** rather than replacing it — the paper's most transferable structural idea.
- **Architecture:** **Two-stage framework — Interpreter + Optimizer** — inside an iterative chat loop indexed by **chat step k = 0, 1, 2, …**
 1. **Interpreter (NLP)** — analyses the user's chat message and converts linguistic expressions into structured specification parameters, emitting an **update marker Δθ ∈ {−1, 0, +1}²** encoding the *direction* of change (increase / decrease / maintain) for each of two parameters. **Two interchangeable Interpreter implementations are built and compared:** (a) **Sentence-BERT with SVM classifiers**, and (b) an **LLM (Meta Llama 3-8b-Instruct)** with a prompt template supporting few-shot pre-labelled samples and JSON output parsed into Δθ.
 2. **Parameter Updater** — applies the update rule to obtain **θ(k+1)**; change magnitude is governed by hyperparameters **η_CPU > 1 and η_LB > 1**, with **[η_CPU, η_LB] = [2, 1.5]** in the reported experiments. Worked example: **θ_LB(2) = η_LB⁻¹ · z_LB(1) = 1.5⁻¹ × 2.0 ms = 1.3 ms**.
 3. **Optimizer** — solves an **ILP** for VM placement and routing subject to resource, latency and capacity constraints parametrized by θ, returning placement **x** and routes **y**. Environmental information (network state) feeds back into the Interpreter, closing the loop.
 **Two updateable specification parameters:** **θ_s^CPU** (minimum CPU resource the user's VM should have) and **θ_s^LB** (latency bound, i.e. maximum communication latency from user s to its VM).
 **Key architectural claim:** because the Optimizer enforces constraints, **feasibility is rigorously guaranteed**, which the authors argue gives *"inherent robustness to LLM hallucinations even without an intent-validation module, as employed in conventional works."* This is deliberately different from the deterministic-routing guardrail in the semantic-router line of work.
- **Control plane / management plane location:** **No named standard control plane is implemented.** The system sits at the **service/infrastructure management layer above virtualized infrastructure**, building explicitly on **NFV** and **IaaS** concepts and positioning itself relative to intent-based networking, citing **3GPP TS 28.312** as background for IBN intent types (deployment, modification, performance assurance, intent report request, feasibility check, regular notification request). **ETSI NFV MANO, ETSI ZSM, ONAP, OSM, O-RAN SMO and Kubernetes control planes are all NOT used.**
- **Inputs and Outputs:** INPUTS: **natural-language chat prompts** in sequence (e.g. *"I no longer need such as plenty CPU"*, *"I would like to have lower latency network"*, *"Get more CPUs, please."*); **network state/environmental information** fed back to the Interpreter; the **current parameter vector θ(k)**; **topology and capacity data** (directed graph of routers and links, link bandwidth φ̄_l, link latency T_l, traffic volume φ_s, datacenter compute capacity); for the LLM Interpreter, **pre-labelled few-shot training samples** (30 in the single-user experiment). OUTPUTS: the **update marker Δθ**; **updated specification parameters θ(k+1)** (CPU demand and latency bound); **VM placement decision x** (x_sd = 1 if user s's VM is placed in datacenter d); **routing decision y** (y_sl = 1 if user s's communication traverses link l); an **infeasibility indication** when the problem under θ is not executable — detected *before* acting, *"which contributes to the preservation of network integrity."*
- **Managed resources:** **Virtual machines (VMs)**, **CPU cores**, **datacenter compute capacity**, **link bandwidth**, **network latency/routing paths**. Memory and storage are named in the introduction as parts of IaaS but are not decision variables. Radio, spectrum, VNFs and pods are not managed.
- **Network domains:** **Cloud/datacenter (multi-DC)** plus the **transport network** interconnecting routers and datacenters — the topology contains **three datacenters** reached over a routed transport topology. RAN and 5G core are not modelled.
- **Interfaces/APIs:** **NOT REPORTED.** No A1, E2, O1, O2, REST, NETCONF/YANG, TMF Open API, CAMARA or Kubernetes API. The "interface" here is a **natural-language chat interface**, not a standards-based programmatic API. Deployment is assumed to occur via the underlying virtualization platform, which is not specified.
- **Optimization engine:** **Integer Linear Programming (ILP)** for virtual network allocation, solving for VM placement x and routing y. Three weighted objectives are minimized: **J₁(x,y) = max_{l∈L} (y_sl φ_s)/φ̄_l** (maximum link utilization — spreads traffic load); **J₂(x,y) = Σ_{s∈S} Σ_{l∈L} y_sl T_l** (total network latency — prevents long routes); **J₃(x,y) = ‖x − x⁻‖₁** (change in VM placement versus the previous chat step, x⁻ = 0 at k = 0 — suppresses sudden placement churn and maintains stability). **Weights used: ω₁ = 1, ω₂ = 0.01, ω₃ = 0.05.** The ILP is solved at each chat step under constraints x, y ∈ 𝒵_θ. **The specific solver/library is NOT REPORTED.**
- **ML/DRL/LLM/agent usage:** **LLM used: Meta Llama 3-8b-Instruct**, role = **intent extraction only** — it consumes a prompt built from a template plus optional few-shot pre-labelled samples and returns JSON parsed into Δθ; it does **not** make allocation decisions. **Baseline ML model: Sentence-BERT + SVM classifiers** for the same intent-extraction task. **No DRL, no RL, no multi-agent system, no fine-tuning.** Adaptation is via **few-shot in-context learning** and the **parameter-update rule**. The paper frames the design as integrating *"the strengths of both paradigms—statistical and model-based approaches."*
- **Centralized vs distributed; single vs multi-domain; closed-loop vs open-loop; static vs dynamic:** **Centralized** — one Interpreter + one Optimizer for the users of a single provider's infrastructure. **Single-domain** (one network, three DCs within one routed topology based on Internet2). **Closed-loop** — explicitly iterative: *"an iterative, loop process where user feedback in natural language updates the virtual network parameters."* **Dynamic** — VM placement and routes are recomputed at each chat step; the J₃ stability term explicitly damps unnecessary reconfiguration, i.e. dynamics are deliberately rate-limited.
- **SLA awareness:** **Partially yes — as a feasibility-enforced constraint rather than an SLA lifecycle.** The **latency bound θ_LB is an explicit, user-adjustable specification parameter** enforced as a hard constraint by the ILP, with feasibility guaranteed before action (and infeasibility detected in advance — demonstrated at k = 3). CPU demand θ_CPU is similarly enforced. **However:** no SLA contract, no SLA class or template, no violation detection/reporting, no penalty model, and no post-deployment SLA monitoring. Report as: **hard constraint enforcement of latency and CPU requirements with guaranteed feasibility; no formal SLA lifecycle.**
- **Network slicing support:** **No.** No slice concept — no slice types, no NSSI/NSI, no isolation, no slice lifecycle. Management granularity is per-user VM plus per-link routing flow.
- **Kubernetes/cloud-native support:** **No** — the VM abstraction is used throughout, with no containers, pods or Kubernetes APIs.
- **Evaluation environment:** **Simulation, not a testbed.** No simulator product is named — the ILP is solved numerically over a modelled topology. **Mininet, Containernet, Kubernetes, Open5GS, OSM, ONAP, OpenAirInterface, Colosseum, 5GinFIRE: none are used.** Topology: *"based on the IP network in Internet2"*, with **L = 26 links**, each link bandwidth **φ̄_l = 1.0 Gbps** and each link latency **T_l = 1.0 ms**; **three datacenters (DC 1, DC 2, DC 3)** and **nine routers**. Traffic: user volume **φ = 0.5 Gbps**. **Single-user case:** user at **Router 3**; initial **θ_CPU(0) = 2 cores**, **θ_LB(0) = 3 ms**; **[η_CPU, η_LB] = [2, 1.5]**; weights **ω₁ = 1, ω₂ = 0.01, ω₃ = 0.05**; **30 pre-labelled examples** for LLM few-shot learning. The three prompts drove: at k = 0 the VM placed in **DC 2 connected to Router 7**; at k = 1 CPU usage declined as prompted; at k = 2 the VM moved to **DC 1 connected to Router 2** with **θ_LB(2) = 1.3 ms**; at k = 3 the problem became **infeasible** and was correctly detected. **Multi-user case:** three users at **Routers 1, 3 and 4**.
- **Network scale:** **26 links, 9 routers, 3 datacenters, 1 user** (single-user experiment) then **3 users** (multi-user experiment). Traffic volume **0.5 Gbps** per user; link capacity **1.0 Gbps**; link latency **1.0 ms**. A small-scale evaluation.
- **Baselines and Metrics:** BASELINES: the only quantitative baseline is the **Sentence-BERT + SVM Interpreter** versus the **LLM Interpreter** for the intent-extraction sub-task. For the allocation problem itself there is **no alternative algorithm baseline** (no greedy, no LP relaxation, no DRL comparison) — the ILP is the reference formulation. Qualitative contrast is made against end-to-end approaches lacking feasibility guarantees. METRICS: **Precision, Recall, Balanced Accuracy** (for change-direction detection, evaluated separately for Δθ_CPU = ±1 and Δθ_LB = ±1), **Syntax error [%]**, **Computation time [s]**, with **training sample size** as the swept independent variable.

 - **Headline comparison (authors' words):** *"the LLM exhibited superior accuracy in distinguishing change direction and adapting to the domain, while the lighter BERT+SVM model maintained a significantly shorter inference time."*
 - **Latency gap:** LLM inference ≈ **8.18-8.55 s** versus BERT+SVM ≈ **4.31×10⁻³-8.81×10⁻³ s** — approximately **three orders of magnitude (~1000×) slower**.
 - **Data efficiency:** *"the LLM demonstrates higher classification performance than BERT+SVM with a small sample size (10 samples for the LLM)"*; the LLM reaches **balanced accuracy 1/1 at 30 samples** while BERT+SVM peaks at **0.93/0.7**.  - **Both extractors over-trigger:** *"both BERT+SVM and LLM tend to have high recall but low precision. This suggests that the Interpreter is successful at capturing prompts that actually require a parameter change, but tends to over-interpret unrelated prompts as if they imply parameter changes."*

How shold I do?"* produced Δθ = [−1 0] (CPU decrease) when the expected result was **Δθ = [0 0]**; the LLM reasoned *"I'm going to assume that the user might be experiencing some performance issues with the virtual machine, which could be related to CPU resources."*
 - **Feasibility behaviour:** in the single-user case the optimizer reported **infeasible at k = 3**, correctly refusing an unsatisfiable request; in the multi-user case the system *"demonstrated the system's ability to operate at the feasibility boundary, where conflicting requests led to infeasibility."*
- **Main limitations:** As stated by the authors: *"The future works include the development of a conflict resolution and negotiation mechanism. Our multi-user scenario experiment demonstrated the system's ability to operate at the feasibility boundary, where conflicting requests led to infeasibility. Therefore, the next step is to design a communication mechanism to ensure that limited resources are shared fairly and to the user's satisfaction."* — i.e. **when multiple users' chat requests conflict, the current system reports infeasibility rather than resolving or negotiating.** Methodological: (1) **tiny scale and no real infrastructure** — 3 DCs, 9 routers, 26 links, at most 3 users, uniform 1.0 Gbps/1.0 ms links, which removes the heterogeneity that makes real placement problems hard; (2) **the LLM is not an orchestrator** — it emits only a 2-dimensional discrete direction vector; all allocation intelligence is in the ILP, so the approach does not scale to intents that cannot be reduced to two scalar parameters; (3) **~8 s LLM latency is disqualifying for interactive or real-time operation**, and the authors do not reconcile it with any operational requirement despite acknowledging BERT+SVM's speed matters for *"real-time network operation"*; (4) **changing the intent task requires re-authoring the output contract and re-labelling** — hard-wired to CPU and latency bound; (5) **low precision is a real operational hazard** — recall is 1.0 almost everywhere but precision drops to 0.4-0.5, so many unrelated prompts are misread as change requests, causing unnecessary reconfigurations only partially mitigated by the soft J₃ penalty (ω₃ = 0.05), not a gate; (6) **no security analysis** — prompt injection, spoofing and authorization are undiscussed; (7) **no comparison against an alternative allocation algorithm**, so the ILP's advantage over heuristics and its scaling behaviour are untested.
- **Research gap this suggests:** (i) **The "LLM proposes, optimizer disposes" pattern is the strongest and most generalisable contribution and is under-exploited** — by making the optimizer the feasibility authority, the framework obtains hallucination robustness *without* a validation module; gap: extending this to **richer intent spaces** (the LLM emitting full constraint sets or objective weights) and characterizing **when the guarantee still holds**. (ii) **Multi-user conflict resolution and fair resource negotiation are explicitly left open** — the moment two tenants' intents cannot both be satisfied, an orchestrator needs arbitration, priority and fairness semantics rather than an infeasibility report; this connects directly to multi-domain and multi-operator orchestration. (iii) **Latency-accuracy Pareto is unexplored** — the numbers clearly suggest a hybrid (fast classifier for routine prompts, LLM escalation for ambiguous ones), but no crossover point is measured; gap: **cascaded/tiered intent interpreters with published latency-accuracy curves**. (iv) **Precision, not recall, is the binding constraint** in a closed reconfiguration loop, yet the metric emphasis is on balanced accuracy; gap: **cost-sensitive evaluation of intent extractors** weighting false triggers by reconfiguration disruption. (v) **Scope stops at VM+link allocation in one provider's DCs** — no slice model, no RAN, no cloud-native substrate, no standard management interface; gap: **embedding feasibility-guaranteed LLM intent handling inside a standards-based orchestration stack** (which Paper 16c attempts in O-RAN with the opposite trade-off).

- **Architecture:** **O-RAN two-timescale split with LLM assistance placed in the Non-RT/SMO domain:**

Roles map to **RMIO** (RAN Management Intent Owner) and **RMIH** (RAN Management Intent Handler), aligning with **ETSI ZSM owner/handler roles**.

(2) **the RAN is simulated, not real** — enforcement terminates at `e2sim-rc`, an extended OSC E2Sim emulating a gNB; no real DU/CU, no real radio, no UE traffic, no over-the-air validation, so claims about meeting "carrier-grade performance standards" are made against an emulator on a 12-vCPU VM; (3) **the CAMARA API is a mock**, so northbound slice-booking integration is not validated against a real CAMARA implementation, a real slice management system or a real 5G core — end-to-end "slice provisioning" stops at simulated PRB parameters; (4) **SLA compliance is never actually measured** — success is defined as *"correct MCP tool invocation and valid A1 policy generation"*, i.e. translation success, not SLA fulfilment; no KPI is observed post-enforcement and no SLA-violation rate is reported, so **the paper's own research question is not answered empirically**; (5) **query models were used as their own judges** — Claude Opus 4.5 is both an evaluated model and the temperature-0 judge, a self-preference risk not controlled for, and MCPUseMetric has no reported human agreement; (6) **no non-LLM baseline**, so it is impossible to tell whether the LLM adds value over deterministic schema mapping, especially since CAMARA schema validation, guardrails and field-population rules are all deterministic components doing much of the correctness work; (7) **SMO-layer latency is very large** (~15 s for LLM tool start alone) and intrinsic to *"the stochastic processing within the LLM components"* — the mitigation is architectural (confine slowness to the SMO) which is sound but does not make intent provisioning fast; (8) **prompt hygiene is fragile** — GPT-5's "delay budget" ambiguity and Claude Sonnet 4.5's 16% over-cautious refusal both stem from prompt/schema interaction, and the hand-tuned eight-rule prompt's robustness outside the curated 100-sample set is untested; (9) **the evaluation dataset was itself LLM-generated** (*"structured into a prompt, along with ORION's operating characteristics, to generate different intent types using GPT-5 in a supervised manner"*), risking bias toward GPT-5's phrasing — a confound the paper does not discuss; (10) **no scale, concurrency or multi-operator evaluation** — number of concurrent slices/cells is not reported, there is no multi-domain federation and no load test beyond the 10,000-intent/day cost extrapolation.
- **Research gap this suggests:** (i) **The SLA claim is architectural, not empirical — and this is the field's central gap.** Across all three Paper-16 works (and Papers 12/13) the pattern repeats: **intent understanding is measured, SLA fulfilment is not.** Gap: **closed-loop, KPI-verified SLA assurance benchmarks for LLM-based orchestration** — reported violation rates, time-to-detect, time-to-remediate and rollback success, on real or high-fidelity RAN infrastructure rather than an E2 simulator. (ii) **MCP tool-orchestration fidelity, not language understanding, is the binding constraint** — Claude Sonnet 4.5 understood intents but failed to call the tool (17% success; 13% classification), and Gemini's serialization produced unverifiable arguments; the paper says *"raw language understanding does not guarantee reliable tool orchestration, a critical consideration for agentic SMO deployments."* Gap: **agentic-interface reliability engineering and benchmarking** — deterministic tool-call contracts, schema-aware retry/repair and serialization-conformance testing — as a first-class problem separate from model capability. (iii) **Cost and latency are now first-class design axes with no standard reporting** — the 156× cost spread and ~15 s SMO-layer latency mean model choice is an operational decision; gap: **cost/latency-normalised evaluation protocols**. (iv) **Answer-set governance for the prediction gap** — Gemini 3 Flash was 100% correct *when it predicted* but predicted only 66% of the time; gap: **explicit abstention/confidence semantics for orchestrator agents**. (v) **Self-evaluation and judge bias** — gap: **validated, human-anchored evaluation rubrics for tool-calling quality in network management**, with inter-rater agreement and non-self judges. (vi) **Benchmark contamination via LLM-generated test data** — generating the gold set with GPT-5 likely advantages GPT-family models; gap: **expert-authored, provenance-controlled telecom intent corpora**. (vii) **The O-RAN/standards path and the optimization path remain disconnected** — ORION is standards-aligned but has no optimizer; Papers 12, 13 and 16b have rigorous optimizers but no standards alignment; gap: **LLM intent front-ends coupled to formal optimizers inside O-RAN/ZSM management planes**.

*"more research should be dedicated to VM allocation, specifically to optimize some objectives which were neglected in comparison to others (e.g., overhead, ROI, and aggregate traffic)."*; *"The pillar is to enable efficient simulation tools (if not real tests such as Verizon's) that could reflect the UE consumption of services, the nature of the heterogeneous infrastructure and the SLA requirements to fulfill."*; and on management context: *"since virtualization enables cloud providers to manipulate directly the virtual equipment … the virtual equipment becomes somehow service agnostic, meaning that it knows almost nothing about its contribution to the applications as a whole, which results in losing the management context capability."* Methodological: (a) **purely narrative/tabular review with no own experiments, no simulation, no prototype, no reproducibility artifact**; (b) **no meta-analysis** — the survey never aggregates its corpus quantitatively, so its comparative tables cannot support quantitative claims about the state of the art; (c) **standards coverage is dated/narrow** — ETSI NFV MANO and OpenStack only, no ETSI ZSM, ONAP, OSM, O-RAN, 3GPP SA5 slicing management, or Kubernetes/cloud-native placement, all of which were already central to operational orchestration by 2018-2019; placement is treated essentially as a cloud-resource-assignment problem; (d) **ML/AI coverage is very thin** (Kalman filter, local weight regression), with no DRL-based or learning-based closed-loop placement despite that being the emerging direction; (e) **VMP and VNFP are reviewed somewhat separately**, so joint VM+VNF placement and its coupling is not a first-class formulation; (f) the authors' own stated benchmark for novelty is a comparison with a single prior survey.
- **Research gap this suggests:** **The single biggest gap this survey exposes:** as of this COMST survey, VNF/VM placement research was **algorithm-centric, single-cloud, offline-dataset-driven, and disconnected from the operational control planes** (no ZSM, no ONAP/OSM, no O-RAN, no cloud-native/Kubernetes placement). Placement was studied as a **static or periodically-replanned optimization problem**, not as a **closed-loop, SLA-governed orchestration function**. Concretely, the survey's own challenges imply: **(i)** placement at **large-scale, multi-tenant** cloud setups (explicitly *"unresolved"*; reviewed solutions' constraints *"don't consider the case of multi-tenant cloud environments"*); **(ii) multi-cloud / federated-cloud** and cross-domain placement (only *"a few"* works; one reviewed solution *"Cannot know if it will be performing well in a federated environment"*); **(iii) reliability and k-resiliency**-aware placement; **(iv) online placement overhead** and **data-transfer-time** optimization; **(v)** under-studied objectives — **overhead, ROI, aggregate traffic**; **(vi)** under-served use cases — **vCPE/SD-WAN, mobile base-station virtualization, CDN virtualization, VPaaS**; **(vii) container placement** (explicitly deferred), and by extension the whole cloud-native/Kubernetes placement problem; **(viii) realistic evaluation environments** bridging simulation and real deployments; **(ix)** preserving **management context** in service-agnostic virtual equipment. Exact-CSP approaches *"cannot be applied to very large datasets"*, and most reviewed work is **offline**: *"most of the work mentioned in the literature is dedicated to offline VMP, except in [100]."* For an orchestrator-focused review, this survey is best cited as evidence that **the placement literature it surveys is *not* orchestrator work** — it supplies type-(B) optimization algorithms an orchestrator *could* consume, which is exactly the (A)-vs-(B) distinction the taxonomy needs.
